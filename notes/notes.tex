\section{Introduction}  \label{sec:intro}

\subsection*{Purpose}  The greatest importance of mathematical and numerical models to science, and glaciology in particular, is \emph{introspection}.  In building and using a model you are asking yourself: When I combine my imperfect and incomplete understanding of glacier processes into this model, does it behave as I expect?  A numerical simulation can only report the consequences of your equations, inadequacies and all, but the approximation can show how processes interact to give overall behavior, and it can sometimes demonstrate flaws in your understanding of those processes.  In any case, while all codes produce numbers, we want numbers that closely-approximate our intended continuum equations.  Therefore the differential equations, and not the computer codes themselves, will be a primary focus of these notes.

\subsection*{Download and Python codes}  These notes, some slides, additional exercises, and  several Python codes are all distributed by cloning the \texttt{git} repository at
\begin{quote}
\url{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy}
\end{quote}
\noindent The codes are in the \texttt{py/} subdirectory.

  \begin{quote}\emph{surface kinematics and shallow approximations of ice flow.}\end{quote}

\section{Ice flow equations}  \label{sec:continuum}

\subsection*{Glacier ice is a weird fluid}  Ice in glaciers behaves as a viscous fluid \cite{GreveBlatter2009}, and we describe its motion by a velocity field $\mathbf{u}$.  If the ice fluid were faster-moving than it is in reality, and if furthermore it were linearly-viscous like liquid water, then it would be a ``typical'' incompressible fluid and we would use the Navier-Stokes partial differential equations as the model \cite{Wesseling2001}:
\begin{align}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} &= 0 &&\text{\emph{incompressibility}} \label{incompressible} \\
\rho \left(\mathbf{u}_t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla \mathbf{u}\right) &= \nabla \cdot (\nu \nabla \mathbf{u}) - \nabla p + \rho \mathbf{g} &&\text{\emph{stress balance}} \label{navierstokes}
\end{align}

Besides the velocity $\bu$, the other symbols in these equations are the ice density $\rho$, the viscosity $\nu$, the pressure $p$, and the acceleration of gravity $\bg$.  The velocity field $\bu$ is divergence-free (equation \eqref{incompressible}), thus no fluid is created or lost within the domain, which is one aspect of mass conservation.  Equation \eqref{navierstokes} is a form of ``$ma=F$'', Newton's second law.  The left side has the acceleration following the fluid, $\mathbf{u}_t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla \mathbf{u}$, while the right-hand side is the total force per unit volume, the sum of viscous, pressure, and gravity ($\rho \mathbf{g} = \left<0,0,-\rho g\right>$) forces, respectively.  Using $\sigma$ to denote the Cauchy stress tensor, the right side can be written $\nabla \cdot \sigma + \rho \mathbf{g}$.

The space of solutions to \eqref{incompressible} and \eqref{navierstokes} is rich.  It includes turbulent flows, which we will not need here.  In fact, reading a book-length introduction to fluids such as \cite{Acheson1990}, or their numerical solution (\emph{computational fluid dynamics}) such as \cite{Wesseling2001}, is overkill for glacier modelling, except for a broader education.  One might even ask: is glacier modelling really computational fluid dynamics at all?  The answer is \emph{yes}.  Glacier ice flow is a large-scale, climate-relevant fluid problem, comparable to ocean circulation for example.  However, the topics which make ocean flows exciting, for example turbulence, convection, coriolis force, and density stratification, are irrelevant here.  What could be interesting about the flow of slow and strong ice?  How does it work, anyway?

\subsection*{Glen-Stokes equations}  We have said that glacier ice is a ``slow'' fluid.  In terms of equation \eqref{navierstokes}, this means that $\rho \left(\mathbf{u}_t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla \mathbf{u}\right) \approx 0$, which is to say the forces (stresses) of inertia are negligible, and Newton's second law becomes a \emph{stress balance}.  However, ice is also a shear-thinning fluid, with ``non-Newtonian'' behavior wherein larger strain rates imply smaller viscosity.  The nonlinear viscosity $\nu$ is not constant, and an empirically-based flow law is needed.  Combining these ideas gives the canonical ice flow model:
\begin{align}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} &= 0 &&\text{\emph{incompressibility}} \label{incompressibleagain} \\
- \nabla \cdot \tau + \nabla p &= \rho \mathbf{g} &&\text{\emph{stress balance}} \label{forcebalance} \\
D\bu &= A |\tau|^2 \tau &&\text{\emph{$n$=3 Glen-Steinemann-Nye flow law}} \label{flowlaw}
\end{align}
Note that $\rho>0$ and $\bg$ are constant, and the \emph{ice softness} $A>0$ is constant for the purposes of our simplified treatment, but the other fields depend on $t,x,y,z$.

In \eqref{flowlaw} the deviatoric stress $\tau$ and strain rate $D\bu$ tensors are proportional, with a scalar power-law coefficient $A|\tau|^2$.  (This is Nye's contribution to the flow law form.)  Note that $|\tau|^2 = (1/2) \tau_{ij} \tau_{ij}$, using the summation convention, which defines the \emph{effective deviatoric stress} $|\tau|$.  Also, by definition $D\bu = \frac{1}{2}(\grad \bu + \grad \bu^\top)$, thus $(D\bu)_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left((u_i)_{x_j}+(u_j)_{x_i}\right)$ in components.  Thus $D\bu$ is symmetric by definition, and it follows from \eqref{incompressibleagain} and \eqref{flowlaw} that both tensors $\tau$, $D\bu$ have trace zero.  At an abstract level, the many possible solutions of \eqref{forcebalance}--\eqref{flowlaw} are constrained by incompressibility \eqref{incompressibleagain}, and the pressure $p$ serves as a Lagrange multiplier for this constraint.

Observe that equations \eqref{incompressibleagain}--\eqref{flowlaw} do not contain any time derivatives!  This implies that the combined effect of glacier geometry, boundary stresses, and gravity will determine the solution velocity and stress fields ($\bu$, $p$, $\tau$)  at each instant \cite{JouvetRappaz2011}.\footnote{Ice flow simulation codes need no memory of prior momentum or velocity.  Said another way, velocity is a ``diagnostic'' output of ice flow models.  Unlike in a Navier-Stokes-based simulation, velocity is not needed for (re)starting a simulation.}  The equations apply at every time, but as the glacier interacts with the climate its geometry and basal stresses will be changing, and thus the Stokes solution fields will also be changing.  In other words, though these equations have no time derivatives, the modelled glacier is  \emph{not} in steady state just because we use them.

Consider the $x,z$-coordinates \emph{planar} (or \emph{flow line}) case.  Now one velocity component in equations \eqref{incompressibleagain}--\eqref{flowlaw} is zero, $v=0$, and derivatives with respect to $y$ are also zero:
\begin{align}
u_x + w_z &= 0 &&\text{\emph{incompressibility}} \label{incompressiblexz} \\
- \tau_{11,x} - \tau_{13,z} + p_x &= 0 &&\text{\emph{stress balance} ($x$)} \label{stokespx} \\
- \tau_{13,x} + \tau_{11,z} + p_z &= - \rho g &&\text{\emph{stress balance} ($z$)} \label{stokespz} \\
u_x &= A \tau^2 \tau_{11} &&\text{\emph{flow law (diagonal)}}  \label{forceflowx} \\
u_z + w _x &= 2 A \tau^2 \tau_{13} &&\text{\emph{flow law (off-diagonal)}} \label{forceflowz}
\end{align}
Here $\tau_{13}$ is a shear stress while $\tau_{11},\tau_{33}$ are deviatoric longitudinal stresses.  In this planar case it follows that $\tau_{33}=-\tau_{11}$, by incompressibility and the flow law, which explains why we can write \eqref{stokespz} without $\tau_{33}$.  Also, a short calculation shows $\tau^2 = \tau_{11}^2+\tau_{13}^2$.  Observe that equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}--\eqref{forceflowz} form a system of 5 nonlinear equations in 5 unknown scalar fields ($u,w,p,\tau_{11},\tau_{13}$).

\subsection*{Slab-on-a-slope solution}  However, equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}--\eqref{forceflowz} are still quite complicated and difficult to understand!  Before jumping into numerical solution methods we consider a simplified situation, a uniform slab of ice on an incline.  Here we can exactly solve the Stokes equations, and the same calculation motivates a later shallow model.

\onefig{slab}{Rotated axes for the slab-on-a-slope calculation.}

We rotate our coordinates only for this example.  The $x,z$ axes shown in Figure \ref{fig:slab} are rotated clockwise at angle $\alpha>0$ so that the gravity vector now has components $\mathbf{g} = (g \sin\alpha,- g \cos \alpha)$.  Equations \eqref{stokespx} and \eqref{stokespz} have the new form
\begin{align}
- \tau_{11,x} - \tau_{13,z} + p_x &= \rho g \sin\alpha, \label{stokespxrot} \\
- \tau_{13,x} + \tau_{11,z} + p_z &= - \rho g \cos\alpha. \label{stokespzrot}
\end{align}

For this uniform slab, assume further that there is no variation with $x$. Then the system of Stokes equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}, \eqref{forceflowx}, \eqref{forceflowz}, \eqref{stokespxrot}, \eqref{stokespzrot} simplifies greatly:
\begin{equation}
w_z = 0, \quad \tau_{11} = 0, \quad u_z = 2 A \tau^2 \tau_{13}, \quad \tau_{13,z} = - \rho g \sin\alpha, \quad p_z = - \rho g \cos\alpha. \label{stokesslab}
\end{equation}
Each unknown is now a function of $z$ only.  Boundary conditions come from assuming that the ice is not crossing the bed and the stress-free ice surface is moving parallel to the $x$-axis.  Thus: $w(0)=0$, $p(H)=0$, $\tau_{13}(H)=0$.  By integrating 3 of the equations in \eqref{stokesslab} with respect to $z$, we get $w(z)=0$, $p(z) = (\rho g \cos\alpha) (H-z)$, and $\tau_{13}(z) = (\rho g \sin\alpha) (H-z)$, and we already have $\tau_{11}(z)=0$.  Note that $H-z$ is the depth below the ice surface; pressure and shear stress are proportional to depth.  Integrating the remaining equation yields the horizontal velocity function, using notation $u(0)=u_0$:
\begin{align}
u(z) &= u_0 + \frac{1}{2} A (\rho g \sin\alpha)^3  \left(H^4 - (H-z)^4\right)  \label{uslab}
\end{align}

Do we believe formula \eqref{uslab}, which makes a specific prediction about the profile of flow in a glacier?  Figure \ref{fig:slabvel} compares observations of a mountain glacier, showing that this model does a credible job of capturing deformation flow in this case.  However, the basal velocity $u_0=u(0)$ is generally unknown.  For simplicity we will assume in these notes that there is no sliding ($u_0=0$), for example because the ice is frozen to the bed, but effective models for glacier sliding are available \cite{BuelerBrown2009}.

\twofigsizes{slabvel}{athabasca-deform}{Left:  Velocity from formula \eqref{uslab}.  Right:  Measured velocity in a glacier (Athabasca Glacier \cite{SavagePaterson1963}).}{2.0in}{1.8in}

In Section \ref{sec:sia} we will convert the severe simplifications of the slab-on-a-slope model into the \emph{shallow ice approximation}.

\subsection*{Forms of the flow law}  Returning to the general case, flow law \eqref{flowlaw} has another important form.  Recall $|\tau|^2 = (1/2) \tau_{ij} \tau_{ij}$, and define $|D\bu|^2 = (1/2) (D\bu)_{ij} (D\bu)_{ij}$.  The scalars $|\tau|$ and $|D\bu|$ are tensor \emph{norms}.  From \eqref{flowlaw} we get $|D\bu| = A |\tau|^3$ so $|\tau| = A^{-1/3} |D\bu|^{1/3}$.  Thus \eqref{flowlaw} can be rewritten
\begin{equation}
\tau = 2 \nu\, D\bu  \qquad \text{\emph{viscosity form of a flow law}} \label{viscosityflowlaw}
\end{equation}
where for the $n=3$ case the (nonlinear) \emph{effective viscosity} $\nu$ has this form:
\begin{equation}
\nu = \frac{1}{2} A^{-1/3} |D\bu|^{-2/3} \label{viscositypowerlaw}
\end{equation}
Often $B = A^{-1/3}$ is called the \emph{ice hardness}.  Equations \eqref{viscosityflowlaw} and \eqref{viscositypowerlaw} eliminate the stress tensor $\tau$ from the stress balance \eqref{forcebalance}, so that the Stokes formulas may use only velocity $\bu$, pressure $p$, and strain rates $D\bu$.

\subsection*{Hydrostatic and Blatter-Pattyn approximations}  A relatively-mild simplification of the Glen-Stokes equations comes from assuming that horizontal variation in the vertical shear stress is small compared to the other terms.  This removes the single term $\tau_{13,x}$ from the $z$-component of the (planar) stress balance \eqref{stokespz}, giving
\begin{equation}
\tau_{11,z} + p_z = - \rho g. \label{hydrostaticpz}
\end{equation}
Because $\sigma_{ij} = \tau_{ij} - p \delta_{ij}$, so that $\tau_{11}+p = - \tau_{33}+p = - \sigma_{33}$, equation \eqref{hydrostaticpz} says that the vertical normal stress $\sigma_{33}$ is linear in depth, that is, it is \emph{hydrostatic}.\footnote{The pressure $p$ itself is not generally hydrostatic in this model.}  Also taking a surface value of $\sigma_{33}=0$ we get
\begin{equation}
\tau_{11}+p = \rho g (s-z), \label{hydrostaticitself}
\end{equation}
where $z=s(t,x)$ is the \emph{surface elevation}.

Equation \eqref{hydrostaticitself} allows us to eliminate the pressure $p$ from the model equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}--\eqref{forceflowz}.  In fact, taking the $x$-derivative of \eqref{hydrostaticitself}, then substituting into \eqref{stokespx} to eliminate $p_x$, and then using the viscosity form \eqref{viscosityflowlaw} leads to this equation \cite{GreveBlatter2009}:
\begin{equation}
\left(4 \nu u_x\right)_x + \left(\nu (u_z+w_x)\right)_z = \rho g s_x \qquad\text{\emph{hydrostatic stress balance}} \label{stresshydrostatic}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}, \eqref{viscositypowerlaw}, \eqref{stresshydrostatic}, plus appropriate boundary conditions, determine $u$ and $w$, without the simultaneous need to solve for $p$.  (The remaining unknowns can be determined by differentiation and/or integration in $z$.)  However, while $p$ is gone, \eqref{stresshydrostatic} remains non-trivially coupled to incompressibility \eqref{incompressiblexz} through the vertical velocity $w$.

We get the even-simpler Blatter-Pattyn model by dropping $w_x$ from equation \eqref{stresshydrostatic}:
\begin{equation}
\left(4 \nu u_x\right)_x + \left(\nu u_z\right)_z = \rho g s_x \qquad\text{\emph{Blatter-Pattyn stress balance}} \label{stressblatter}
\end{equation}
Now solving \eqref{viscositypowerlaw}, \eqref{stressblatter} as a boundary value problem yields the scalar horizontal velocity $u$.  One may then recover $w$ using integration in $z$ on incompressibility \eqref{incompressiblexz}.

Because it has a form similar to the well-understood Poisson equation $u_{xx} + u_{zz} = f$ \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005}, the process of solving equation \eqref{stressblatter} may be more familiar than \eqref{stresshydrostatic}.  However, the omission of $w_x$ from the stress balance, a mechanism which smooths the surface of a glacier once the model is made time-dependent (next!), is significant.  The hydrostatic and Blatter-Pattyn stress balances give different predictions.


\section{Surface kinematical equation (evolution of glaciers)}   \label{sec:masscont}

\subsection*{Time-dependent ice domain}  The Glen-Stokes stress balance model for ice flow, and its approximations, are incomplete as models for glacier behavior in a very important sense.  They describe the ice flow at every instant but they give no direct clue about how glaciers \emph{change}.  The stress balance equations contain no time derivatives, and they can not directly ``see'' the climate around the glacier,\footnote{In fact the stress balance ``sees'' basal stress as a boundary condition, and this is sometimes regarded as a climate input, but via highly-nontrivial additional models.} other than gravity.

On the other hand, the \emph{domain} on which the stress balance equations are solved evolves in time.  We need notation for this time-dependent geometry.  Let $s(t,x,y)$ be the ice surface elevation in 3D; the mathematical surface itself is $z=s(t,x,y)$.  We assume for now that the glacier bed elevation $b(x,y)$ is time-independent.\footnote{Erosion and earth deformation (e.g.~glacial isostasy) processes would make the bed elevation time-dependent, in which case a \emph{basal kinematical equation} \eqref{basekine} is also needed.}  Our preferred stress balance differential equations are then solved on the 3D set
\begin{equation}
\left\{(x,y,z)\,:\,b(x,y) < z < s(t,x,y)\right\}, \label{domain}
\end{equation}
the changing set of points between the surface and the bed.  In glacier equations it is primarily the ice domain \eqref{domain} that evolves in time, while the stress balance equations are instantaneous truths which hold on this evolving domain.

The figure on the cover page shows notation in the planar (2D) case: $z=s(t,x)$, $z=b(x)$, and $\left\{(x,z)\,:\,b(x) < z < s(t,x)\right\}$ (labeled \textsf{ice} in the figure).

\subsection*{Surface kinematics}  The upper surface evolves in part through the \emph{surface mass balance} (SMB) rate, which is denoted $a(t,x,y)$ or $a(t,x)$ and is also known as the \emph{climatic} mass balance \cite{Cogleyetal2011}.  (``SMB'' is one of a few abbreviations used in these notes; see Table \ref{tab:abbrev}.)  By definition this is the balance between accumulating snow and the comparatively-rapid\footnote{In these notes, and in much of the glacier literature, the velocity of liquid runoff is assumed to be so much larger than the ice flow velocity that the runoff acts instantaneously.} loss of liquid water, by melt and runoff of the snow, firn, or ice, from the upper surface.  Our elevations are in meters, and time is in seconds, so the SMB function $a$ here is in ice-equivalent meters per second.

These notes only consider simulations of land-based glaciers, and we omit models of basal melt, but such melt would contribute to an additional \emph{basal} mass balance rate.  It can be of either sign because of \emph{freeze-on}, e.g.~in floating ice shelves.

The upper surface evolves according to the \emph{surface kinematical equation} (SKE; \cite{SchoofHewitt2013}), which in the planar case is the equation
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} = a + w -u \frac{\partial s}{\partial x}. \label{skeplanar}
\end{equation}

Regarding the notation, the planar ice velocity is $\bu=\left<u,w\right>$, and the variables $u,w$ in \eqref{skeplanar} are these component values at the upper surface, namely $u=u(t,x,s(t,x))$ and $w=w(t,x,s(t,x))$, i.e.~at $z=s(t,x)$.

Equation \eqref{skeplanar} says that the ice surface moves up and down at the rate $\partial s/\partial t$ according to three contributions, the SMB $a$, the vertical ice velocity $w$, and the product of horizontal ice velocity $u$ with the surface slope $\partial s/\partial x$.  Dimensionally, at least, this is correct; $\partial s/\partial x$ is unit-less, and all terms in \eqref{skeplanar} are in meters per second.

Vector notation is convenient when extending \eqref{skeplanar} to 3D, so that the surface elevation function $s(t,x,y)$ depends on two horizontal variables.  Let
\begin{equation}
\bn_s = \Big<-\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}, -\frac{\partial s}{\partial y}, \,1\Big)> \label{normal}
\end{equation}
be an upward-pointing normal vector on the surface.  Then the SKE is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} - \bu \cdot \bn_s = a  \label{ske}
\end{equation}
where $\bu=\left<u,v,w\right>$ (Table \ref{tab:notation}) is evaluated at the surface.  With all terms involving the unknown surface elevation $s$ on the left, form \eqref{ske} suggests we might think of the climate $a$ as given data, and we might try to solve the SKE for $s$, which is exactly what we are about to do.

The SKE is described in glaciers literature as a mere boundary condition \cite{GreveBlatter2009,SchoofHewitt2013}, but it is actually the most important statement of mass conservation in a glacier model.  It can be derived from conservation by a standard ``pillbox'' calculus argument \cite{Aschwandenetal2012}.

\subsection*{First numerical glacier model}  Regarding equation \eqref{skeplanar} or \eqref{ske} one might say:
\begin{center}
\emph{by numerically solving this equation for $s$, one has a model for a glacier.}
\end{center}
Perhaps it is better to say that the SKE is \emph{correct} in a physical sense and can be \emph{understood} as a glacier model.  We will demonstrate it as a minimal model.

The plan is to numerically solve the planar SKE \eqref{skeplanar} for the surface elevation $s(t,x)$.  To do this we make the bold assumptions that the SMB function $a(t,x)$, \emph{and} the surface values of the velocity components, denoted $u(t,x)$ and $w(t,x)$, are given by other agents.  In practice the SMB could be the output of a sub-model, such as a mass/energy process model coupled to atmosphere physics and data \cite{vanderVeen2013}.  The surface velocity components $u,w$ would likewise be the output of a stress balance sub-model (Sections \ref{sec:continuum} and \ref{sec:sia}).  However, the stress balance sub-model depends on the surface elevation $s$ itself, which determines the ice domain geometry,\footnote{The SMB could also depend on $s$, another form of coupling.} and so in fact the whole problem is non-trivially \emph{coupled}, but we must start somewhere.

This first numerical model is in a short Python program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface1d.py}{\texttt{py/surface1d.py}}.  (See the Download part of Section \ref{sec:intro}.)  It generates an approximation to the surface elevation $s(t,x)$, over intervals of space $0<x<L$ and time $0<t<T$, according to \eqref{skeplanar}, by an \emph{explicit} time-dependent numerical method.  We'll use this first program to consider questions of accuracy and stability, and practicalities like verification.

\subsection*{Finite differences}  The program is based on \emph{finite difference} (FD) approximations \cite{LeVeque2007, MortonMayers2005}, which need an introduction on their own.

From calculus, Taylor's theorem says that for a smooth function $f(x)$ and a real number $\Delta$, we have an expansion:
\begin{equation}
f(x+\Delta) = f(x) + f'(x) \Delta + \frac{1}{2} f''(x) \Delta^2 + \frac{1}{3!} f'''(x) \Delta^3 + \dots  \label{taylor}
\end{equation}
(The precise theorem uses a finite sum and estimates the remainder, but a loose version will suffice here.)  One may replace ``$\Delta$'' by its negative, or other multiples, to get related forms such as $f(x-\Delta) = f(x) - f'(x) \Delta + \frac{1}{2} f''(x) \Delta^2 - \dots$  FD numerical models are constructed by combining such expressions to approximate the derivatives in the continuum equations.  The resulting algebraic equations relate unknown gridded values via mere arithmetic, which digital computers can do well.

As a simple example, subtract $f(x)$ from both sides of expansion \eqref{taylor}, then divide by $\Delta$, to find an approximation of the first derivative,
	$$\frac{f(x+\Delta) - f(x)}{\Delta} \approx f'(x).$$
This is usually written to emphasize the size of the remaining terms in the expansion, or more precisely the size of the remainder in Taylor's theorem:
	$$f'(x) = \frac{f(x+\Delta) - f(x)}{\Delta} + O(\Delta).$$
Here $O(\Delta)$ denotes a quantity with magnitude smaller than a fixed multiple of $\Delta$, asymptotically as $\Delta \to 0$.

To apply this FD approach to a \emph{partial} differential equation like SKE \eqref{skeplanar}, let us choose a time step $\Delta t>0$ and a grid spacing $\Delta x>0$.  Apply Taylor expansions in each variable to approximate the partial derivatives in the equation:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}(t,x) &= \frac{s(t+\Delta t,x) - s(t,x)}{\Delta t} + O(\Delta t), \label{fd:partials} \\
\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(t,x) &= \frac{s(t,x+\Delta x) - s(t,x-\Delta x)}{2\Delta x} + O((\Delta x)^2). \notag
\end{align}
The first formula is called a \emph{forward difference} and the second a \emph{centered difference}.

Our numerical solution will use a spatial grid $\{x_j\}$, with equal spacing $\Delta x$ and an integer index $j$.  It will use a temporal grid $\{t_n\}$ with (possibly unequal) spacing $\Delta t$.  We seek a new surface elevation $s(t_{n+1},x_j)$ from some current values $s(t_n,x_{\bullet})$.

Actually, one should distinguish between the continuum solution value $s(t_n,x_j)$, by evaluating the exact solution of the continuum SKE \eqref{skeplanar} at a grid point, and our numerical approximation of it at the same point, now denoted $s_j^n$.  Conceptually, $s_j^n \approx s(t_n,x_j)$, but the values $s_j^n$ are the ones we actually get by computation.  Finally, we will declutter the grid notation by writing $s_j$ for $s_j^n$ and $\snew_j$ for $s_j^{n+1}$.

We all these conventions, consider replacements \eqref{fd:partials}, the FD approximations
    $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} \approx \frac{\snew_j - s_j}{\Delta t}, \qquad \frac{\partial s}{\partial x} \approx \frac{s_{j+1} - s_{j-1}}{2\Delta x}.$$
They convert the SKE \eqref{skeplanar} into a discrete and algebraic equation:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\snew_j - s_j}{\Delta t} = a_j + w_j - u_j \frac{s_{j+1} - s_{j-1}}{2\Delta x}. \end{equation*}
This is intended to be a line of code which determines $\snew_j$, so we rewrite it that way:
\begin{equation}
\snew_j = s_j - \Delta t\, u_j \frac{s_{j+1}-s_{j-1}}{2\Delta x} + \Delta t (a_j + w_j). \label{fd:ftcs}
\end{equation}
Note that this scheme uses 3 values at the current time to determine one value at the new time, and a \emph{stencil} diagram captures this meaning (Figure \ref{fig:stencils}, left).  The standard name for scheme \eqref{fd:ftcs} is \emph{forward-time, centered-space}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\input{tikz/ftcs.tex} \quad \input{tikz/upwind.tex}
\caption{To compute $\snew_j=s_j^{n+1}$ (circle), scheme \eqref{fd:ftcs} (left) uses 3 gridded values at the current time $t_n$ (solid), while the upwind scheme \eqref{fd:upwind} (right; $u_j>0$ case) uses just 2, depending on the flow direction.}
\label{fig:stencils}
\end{figure}

However, if we run a simulation based on scheme \eqref{fd:ftcs} then we will see wiggles which do not go away.  The possibly-surprising remedy is to revert to a less-accurate and asymmetric FD choice for the spatial derivative \cite{MortonMayers2005}.  For $\partial s/\partial x$ we take
    $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(t,x) = \frac{s(t,x) - s(t,x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x} + O(\Delta x)$$
at grid points $(t_n,x_j)$ where $u_j>0$, and otherwise ($u_j \le 0$)
    $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(t,x) = \frac{s(t,x+\Delta x) - s(t,x)}{\Delta x} + O(\Delta x).$$
This is called \emph{upwind} differencing.  These formulas have only first-order accuracy, versus the centered-difference before, but they permit (conditional) stability and the avoidance of wiggles.  Written like \eqref{fd:ftcs}, the upwind scheme is
\begin{equation}
\snew_j = s_j - \Delta t\, \frac{u_j}{\Delta x} \left\{\begin{matrix} s_j-s_{j-1}, & u_j > 0 \\ s_{j+1}-s_j, & u_j \le 0 \end{matrix}\right\} + \Delta t (a_j + w_j).  \label{fd:upwind}
\end{equation}
The stencil in Figure \ref{fig:stencils} right shows the $u_j>0$ case.  In this case the velocity feeds the ice from the left, which explains the ``upwind'' naming.

\subsection*{Time-stepping implementation}  We can now look at the code.  Upwind scheme \eqref{fd:upwind} for SKE \eqref{skeplanar} is a short Python function in the program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface1d.py}{\texttt{py/surface1d.py}}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
def explicitupwindstep(x, s, dt):
    '''compute a time step of the SKE, assuming u(x)>0 always,
    by upwind scheme; left-end value snew[0] = s[0] is unmodified'''
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    snew = s.copy()
    snew[1:] -= dt * u(x[1:]) * (s[1:] - s[:-1]) / dx
    snew[1:] += dt * (a(x[1:]) + w(x[1:]))
    return snew
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent In detail, \texttt{x}, \texttt{s}, and \texttt{snew} are one-dimensional NumPy \cite{Harrisetal2020} arrays, and \texttt{x} is assumed to hold an equally-spaced grid.  Separate ``data'' functions must define $a(x)$, $u(x)$, and $w(x)$.  In this implementation we need the formula for $u(x)$ to be positive, but the two cases in \eqref{fd:upwind} would be easily handled by a conditional statement.  The fact that \texttt{snew[0] = s[0]} never changes is a \emph{boundary condition} compatible with the fact that the velocity is inward at the left end (\texttt{u(x[0])}$>0$).  Also note that the left end of our glacier has zero thickness, that is, \texttt{s[0] = b(0)}.

The rest of the code \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface1d.py}{\texttt{py/surface1d.py}} is worth a look, but there is no need to print it here.  It contains formulas for the data functions, a time-stepping loop, and calls to \texttt{matplotlib} to generate figures.  Various default choices are made for convenience and illustration, for example $\Delta t=1$ a and $\Delta x=1$ km.

Please run the code, and use an image viewer on the simulation results, as follows:

\subsection*{Stability and CFL}  These first results look good (and boring).  However, easily-made modifications reveal stability issues, exposing high-spatial-frequency, rapidly-growing oscillations.  In particular, each of these separate modifications, which are a recommended exercise, generate such exploding wiggles:
\begin{itemize}
\item increase velocity: \quad $u(x)$ \, $\to$ \, $2.5\, u(x)$
\item increase resolution: \quad $\Delta x=1000$ m \, $\to$ \, $\Delta x=400$ m
\item lengthen time-steps: \quad $\Delta t = 1$ a \, $\to$ \, $\Delta t = 2.5$ a    
\end{itemize}

This kind of instability is well understood, and in fact the condition to avoid it has been known for 100 years.  The idea is that in the SKE \eqref{skeplanar}, and similar \emph{advection} equations, information about the surface travels at the speeds $\{u_j\}$.  Therefore we need to make sure that information does not travel more than one spatial distance $\Delta x$ during the time step $\Delta t$.  Equivalently, the maximum dimensionless Courant, Friedrichs, \& Lewy (CFL; \cite{CFL1928}) ratio should be at most one:
\begin{equation}
\frac{(\max_j |u_j|) \Delta t}{\Delta x} \le 1.  \label{cfl}
\end{equation}
The above destabilizing modifications each pushed this ratio over the limit.

In practice, inequality \eqref{cfl} is used to set the time step $\Delta t$ to assure stability.  At run time a simulation program sets $\Delta t = C \frac{\Delta x}{\max_j |u_j|}$, using $C=0.9$ for example, so that \eqref{cfl} holds, even when the surface velocities $u_j$ are changing in time.

\subsection*{Admissibility}  A different type of modification also breaks our method.  In the default set-up, the function $a(x)$ in \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface1d.py}{\texttt{py/surface1d.py}} is linear and decreasing  in $x$, with a zero, i.e.~an equilibrium line \cite{Cogleyetal2011}, at $1/5$ of the total length $L$.  That is, accumulation occurs over $0<x<L/5$ and ablation over $L/5<x<L$.  The modification
\begin{itemize}
\item stronger SMB: \quad $a(x)$ \, $\to$ \, $3 a(x)$  
\end{itemize}
therefore strengthens the ablation over most of the domain.  The final-time result is shown in Figure \ref{fig:inadmissdemo}, with a glacier surface which goes below the bed.  This is not good.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figs/inadmissframe050.png}
\caption{An inadmissible state, with $s<b$ in places, from stronger ablation.  See inequality \eqref{admissible}, and compare Figure \ref{fig:demo}.}
\label{fig:inadmissdemo}
\end{figure}

The bed elevation $b$ does not even appear in equations \eqref{skeplanar} and \eqref{ske}, so it would seem to be irrelevant when using the SKE to evolve the glacier geometry.  However, glaciers can end \emph{where they have ablated away}, and we need to model this real effect.

We say that a surface elevation at some time $t$ is \emph{admissible} if
\begin{equation}
s(t,x,y) \ge b(x,y) \label{admissible}
\end{equation}
for every point $x,y$ in the computational domain.  Equivalently, admissibility says that $H(t,x,y) \ge 0$; a glacier's thickness $H=s-b$ cannot be negative.

The above strengthened-SMB simulation started from an admissible state (Figure \ref{fig:demo}, top), but it evolved to an inadmissible one (Figure \ref{fig:inadmissdemo}).  Avoiding this possibility, at least in an explicit solution method, is quite easy.  Namely, we should \emph{truncate} the solution, $\snew_j = \max\{b_j, \snew_j\}$ (planar case), to force admissibility.

Inequality \eqref{admissible} complements definition \eqref{domain} of the ice domain, and it implies the \emph{free-boundary} nature of the glacier geometry problem \cite{Bueler2016,Bueler2021conservation}.  Bed elevation data $b$ can be needed even when modelling glacier surfaces using the SKE, and it is necessary when solving stress balances (Sections \ref{sec:continuum} and \ref{sec:numericalsia}).  Note that in unglaciated areas it makes sense to say (allow) $s(t,x)=b(x)$, and this is compatible with how digital elevation models work in practice; the surface elevation is the top of the glacier if one is present, otherwise it is the top of the land.

\subsection*{Steady state}  We are regarding the SKE as a minimal model which can predict glacier geometry given data for SMB and surface velocity, and this approach works in \emph{steady state} as well.  Equation \eqref{skeplanar} with $\partial s/\partial t=0$ becomes
\begin{equation}
u \frac{\partial s}{\partial x} = a + w, \label{skeplanarsteady}
\end{equation}
and \eqref{ske} becomes $-\bu \cdot \bn_s = a$.  (Recall notation: $\bu = \left<u,v,w\right>$, $\bn_s = \big<\!-\!\frac{\partial s}{\partial x},-\,\frac{\partial s}{\partial y},1\big>$.)  Then, using an upwind FD idea like \eqref{fd:upwind}, solving \eqref{skeplanarsteady} is easier than the time-dependent case.  Specifically, because $u(x)>0$ everywhere, our FD scheme is
\begin{equation}
u_j \frac{s_{j} - s_{j-1}}{\Delta x} = a_j + w_j \qquad (\text{assumes } u_j>0), \label{fd:upwindsteady}
\end{equation}
or $s_{j} = s_{j-1} + (\Delta x/u_j) (a_j + w_j)$ as an update formula in increasing spatial indices $j$.

In code, we start from the left and have a single \texttt{for} loop:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
s = x.copy()
s[0] = b(0.0)
for j in range(1, J+1):
    s[j] = s[j-1] + (dx / u(x[j])) * (a(x[j]) + w(x[j]))
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent In fact, the same program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface1d.py}{\texttt{py/surface1d.py}} has done the job already.  Viewing the output image (\texttt{output/steady.png}) shows a result very similar to Figure \ref{fig:demo}, bottom.

We have not assured admissibility in the above loop, but this is straightforward using truncation.  The update formula inside the loop becomes
\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
    s[j] = max(b(x[j]), s[j-1] + (dx / u(x[j])) * (a(x[j]) + w(x[j])))
\end{lstlisting}

Solving the same steady-state problem in two horizontal dimensions (a 3D glacier), however, requires completely rethinking the solution strategy, because of the admissibility inequality.  For that we have written a very different, and more advanced, code \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface2d.py}{\texttt{py/surface2d.py}}, which calls the Firedrake finite element library.  Now admissibility \eqref{admissible} is integrated into the partial differential equation problem itself as a \emph{variational inequality} \cite[Chapter 12]{Bueler2021}.  The reader is encouraged to explore this example.

\subsection*{Mass continuity equation}  Equation \eqref{ske}, the SKE in its cleanest form, relates more-or-less observable quantities on a glacier's surface.  For example, it is a preferred equation for relating measured surface velocity to SMB.  However, a different equation is often used to describe the evolution of glacier geometry, namely the \emph{mass continuity} equation, derived next.  It is essentially equivalent to \eqref{ske} because of incompressibility \eqref{incompressible}, which is mass conservation as it applies within the ice.

The derivation is clearest if we state the \emph{basal kinematical equation} first.  Consider the mass balance $m(t,x,y)$ at the basal surface $z=b(t,x,y)$, the difference between melt and freeze-on.  (Here all quantities can be time-dependent.)  The field $m$, in ice-equivalent meters per second, is positive if there is net melt.  It can be of either sign for grounded glaciers, and underneath floating ice shelves, often through thermodynamical effects.  Similar to \eqref{ske}, this kinematical equation is
\begin{equation}
b_t - \bu \cdot \bn_b = m,  \label{bke}
\end{equation}
where $\bn_b = \big<-\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}, -\frac{\partial b}{\partial y}, 1\big>$ is normal to the base and pointing upward, and ``$\bu$'' here denotes $\bu(t,x,y,b(t,x,y))$ (on the base surface).

If the bed elevation $b$ is time-independent ($b_t=0$) then \eqref{bke} relates the ice velocity at the base to the mass balance $m$.  For example, in the case of a grounded glacier with a flat ($\grad b=\big<\frac{\partial b}{\partial x},\frac{\partial b}{\partial y}\big>=0$) and stationary bedrock, and a melting base ($m>0$), the vertical ice velocity at the bed surface is downward: $w=-m$.

Now consider the vector horizontal velocity $\bU = \left<u,v\right>$; contrast notation for the 3D velocity $\bu=\left<u,v,w\right>$.  The horizontal \emph{ice flux} is defined to be the integral
\begin{equation}
\bq(t,x,y) = \int_{b(t,x,y)}^{s(t,x,y)} \bU(t,x,y,z)\,dz,  \label{flux}
\end{equation}
or simply $\bq=\int_b^s\bU\,dz$ if we suppress variables.  The flux is a vector with two components, $\bq=\left<q_1,q_2\right>$.  It is often written $\bq=\bar\bU H$ where $H=s-b$ is the ice thickness; it follows that $\bar\bU=H^{-1} \int_b^s\bU\,dz$ is the vertically-averaged horizontal velocity.

Finally we can state the mass continuity equation itself,
\begin{equation}
    H_t + \Div \bq = a - m.  \label{masscont}
\end{equation}
Note that if the bed is stationary ($b_t=0$) then we can equivalently write ``$s_t$'' on the left, but the most common way to think about this equation is as describing how a layer of ice changes thickness, and thus volume, instead of in terms of elevation.  The divergence here is in horizontal variables, so $\Div\bq= \frac{\partial q_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial q_2}{\partial y}$.

The net function $a-m$ on the right is the \emph{climatic-basal mass balance} \cite{Cogleyetal2011}.  This combination of the surface mass balances $a,m$ determines how much mass is lost or gained in a column within the glacier.

Since equation \eqref{masscont} looks rather different from the others, it makes sense to give the brief derivation.  We need the \emph{Leibniz rule} for differentiating integrals,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_{g(x)}^{f(x)} k(x,y)\,dy\right) = k(x,f(x))\, f'(x) - k(x,g(x)) \, g'(x) + \int_{g(x)}^{f(x)} \frac{\partial k}{\partial x}(x,y)\,dy,  \label{leibniz}
\end{equation}
where $x$ should be regarded as a generic independent variable, and $k,f,g$ as generic differentiable functions.  Note that ``$y$'' is an index (variable) of integration, thus the results do not depend on it.

We apply the Leibniz rule to compute the gradient (partial derivatives) of the flux \eqref{flux}, paying attention to which functions depend on which independent variables:
\begin{align*}
(\Div \bq) (t,x,y) &= \bU(t,x,y,s(t,x,y)) \cdot \grad s(t,x,y) - \bU(t,x,y,b(t,x,y)) \cdot \grad b(t,x,y) \\
   &\qquad + \int_{b(t,x,y)}^{s(t,x,y)} (\Div \bU)(t,x,y,z)\,dz.
\end{align*}
Divergences $\Div$ here are in the horizontal variables $x,y$.  In fact, by incompressibility \eqref{incompressible} we can write the integrand as a vertical derivative of the vertical velocity:
    $$\Div \bU = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = - \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}.$$
Thus, continuing the calculation, indicating surface values by ``$\bU|_s$'' etc., suppressing variables, and applying the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have
    $$\Div \bq = \bU|_s \cdot \grad s - \bU|_b \cdot \grad b - \int_b^s \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\,dz = \bU|_s \cdot \grad s - \bU|_b \cdot \grad b - w|_s + w|_b.$$
The expressions on the right are familiar from the kinematical equations \eqref{ske} and \eqref{bke}, and in fact if we use the 3D velocity $\bu=\left<u,v,w\right>$, and our normal vector notations, and those kinematical equations, then we have
    $$\Div \bq = -\bu|_s \cdot \bn_s + \bu|_b \cdot \bn_b = a - s_t + b_t - m.$$
This says $\Div \bq = (a-m) - H_t$, which is mass continuity \eqref{masscont}.

At this point we have three equations which might be used to describe the evolving geometry of a glacier:
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\emph{\roman{enumi})}}
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item the surface kinematical equation (SKE) \eqref{ske},
  \item the basal kinematical equation \eqref{bke}, and
  \item the mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont}.  
  \end{enumerate}
We derived the third using the first two and incompressibility.  Actually, any \emph{pair} of these three equations implies the third, using incompressibility and the Leibniz rule to make the connections.

In designing a numerical glacier simulation, which equations to use for time-dependent geometry is largely a matter of taste and tradition.  The most common choice in the literature is to use mass continuity \eqref{masscont} as the primary equation, and to interpret the basal kinematical relation \eqref{bke} in a somewhat ad hoc manner.  Our main point here is that one can understand these equations as tools, and be free to use them.

\subsection*{Understanding planar mass continuity}  It helps to illustrate mass continuity in the same $x,z$ flow-line plane we used earlier.  Suppose for simplicity that the base is flat ($b=0$), so the thickness and surface elevation are the same thing ($H=s$), and suppose there is no basal mass balance ($m=0$).  In this planar case the horizontal velocity is a scalar function $U$, and its vertical average $\bar U = H^{-1} \int_0^{H} U\,dz$ and the flux $q = \int_0^{H} U\,dz = \bar U\, H$ are also scalar.

The ice flux $q = \bar U\, H$ is the rate of mass flow through the sides of a part of the glacier, for example at locations $x_1$ and $x_2$ as shown in Figure \ref{fig:slabmasscontfig}.  Therefore mass can be added by SMB $a$, distributed along the upper surface, or by a \emph{difference} in the flux between the left and right sides.  That is, the flow-line area $A$ of this part of the glacier changes according to the sum of certain boundary contributions:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dA}{dt} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} a\,dx + \bar U_1\, H_1 - \bar U_2\, H_2. \label{masscontintegrated}
\end{equation}
In three-dimensions, \eqref{masscontintegrated} would become an equation for $dV/dt$, the rate of change of the volume of a part of the glacier, which would have more sides.

\onefigsize{slabmasscontfig}{Mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont} can be interpreted as giving the rate of change of the time-dependent area $A$.}{2.0in}

Equation \eqref{masscontintegrated} is an integrated form of mass continuity.  To determine the derivative (and planar) form, suppose the width $\Delta x=x_2-x_1$ is small, thus $A\approx \Delta x\, H$.  If we divide by \eqref{masscontintegrated} by $\Delta x$ and take $\Delta x \to 0$ then we get
\begin{equation}
H_t = a - \left(\bar U\, H\right)_x \label{masscontplanar}
\end{equation}
because $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (\int_{x_1}^{x_2} a\,dx)/\Delta x = a$ is the average value of the SMB over the small interval $[x_1,x_2]$, and $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (\bar U_2\, H_2 - \bar U_1\, H_1)/\Delta x = (\bar U\,H)_x$ is a (partial) derivative.  We have re-derived, and perhaps understood better, equation \eqref{masscont} in the planar case.


\section{Shallow ice approximation}   \label{sec:sia}

Ice sheet flow has four outstanding properties as a fluids problem.  Ice sheets are
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\emph{\roman{enumi}})}
\begin{enumerate}
\item slow,
\item shallow,
\item non-Newtonian (shear-thinning), and
\item subject to widespread contact slip (basal sliding).
\end{enumerate}
The first numerical ice flow model in these notes accounts for the first three properties only.  It is the non-sliding, isothermal \emph{shallow ice approximation} (SIA).

Regarding shallowness (\emph{ii}), Figure \ref{fig:green-transect} shows both a no-vertical-exaggeration cross-section of Greenland at $71^\circ$ and the standard vertically-exaggerated version which is more familiar in the glaciological literature.  Without vertical exaggeration we see the ice sheet as a thin blanket, barely three-dimensional.  This illustrates that ice sheets are shallow.  Note, however, that the portion of an ice sheet which you seek to model may not be shallow.

Our earlier slab-on-a-slope example can be converted into a rough derivation of the plane-flow SIA.  We vertically integrate velocity formula \eqref{uslab} in the $u_0=0$ case to get
\begin{equation}
\bar U H = \int_0^H \frac{1}{2} A (\rho g \sin\alpha)^3  \left(H^4 - (H-z)^4\right)\,dz = \frac{2}{5} A (\rho g \sin\alpha)^3 H^5. \label{siaubar}
\end{equation}
Regarding the surface slope, note $\sin \alpha \approx \tan\alpha = - h_x$ when $\alpha$ is a small angle.  Combining these statements with mass continuity \eqref{masscont1D} gives
\begin{equation}
  H_t = M + \left(\frac{2}{5} (\rho g)^3 A H^5 |h_x|^2 h_x\right)_x. \label{sia1D}
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{sia1D} is the SIA equation for nonsliding plane flow.  Noting $h=H+b$, it models the evolution of an ice sheet's thickness $H$.  The model should, however, be solved subject to the constraint that the thickness is positive ($H\ge 0$) \cite{Bueler2016,JouvetBueler2012}.  For careful SIA derivations, which reduce the Stokes equations under a small depth-to-width ratio assumption, follow the Notes and References at the end.

We now state the complete SIA model in preparation for numerical solutions in section \ref{sec:numericalsia}.  Let $\mathbf{U} = (u,v)$ be the vector horizontal velocity; this is a 2D vector-valued function of 3D coordinates $(x,y,z)$.  The SIA shear stress approximation is $(\tau_{13},\tau_{23}) \approx - \rho g (h-z) \nabla h$, and equation \eqref{flowlaw} gives a formula for shear strain rates
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf{U}_z = 2 A |(\tau_{13},\tau_{23})|^{n-1} (\tau_{13},\tau_{23}) = - 2 A (\rho g)^n (h-z)^n |\nabla h|^{n-1} \nabla h.
\end{equation*}
By integrating vertically we get, in the non-sliding case,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{U} = - \frac{2 A (\rho g)^n}{n+1} \left[H^{n+1} - (h-z)^{n+1}\right] |\nabla h|^{n-1} \nabla h.  \label{siavelocity}
\end{equation}
Mass continuity in 2D, which generalizes the 1D version \eqref{masscont1D}, says:
\begin{equation}
    H_t = M - \Div\left(\bar{\mathbf{U}} H\right)
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{masscont} may be written $H_t = M - \Div \bq$ in terms of the map-plane flux $\bq = \int_{b}^{h} \mathbf{U}\,dz = \bar{\mathbf{U}}\,H$.  Note $\bar{\mathbf{U}}$ and $\bq$ are functions of 2D coordinates $(x,y)$ only.  Combining equations \eqref{siavelocity} and \eqref{masscont}, we get an equation for the rate of thickness change in terms of mass balance $M$, thickness $H$, and surface gradient $\grad h$:
\begin{equation}
H_t = M + \Div \left(\Gamma H^{n+2} |\grad h|^{n-1} \grad h \right). \label{sia}
\end{equation}
We have defined the positive constant $\Gamma = 2 A (\rho g)^n / (n+2)$ only to improve appearance.

Equation \eqref{sia} is the SIA in 2D.  Recalling our earlier promise, if we can solve \eqref{sia} numerically then we have a usable model for certain ice sheets.  Mary-Anne Mahaffy \cite{Mahaffy1976} used it in a 1976 numerical model of the Barnes ice cap in Canada, a particularly-simple ice sheet on a rather flat bed.  This was the start of numerical ice sheet modeling.

\subsection*{Analogy with diffusion equations}  Numerical methods for solving \eqref{sia} are modifications of methods for the better-known heat equation, e.g.~ $T_t = D T_{xx}$ for the temperature of a conducting rod, its simplest 1D form.  This model applies when material properties are constant and there are no heat sources.  The positive constant $D$ is the ``diffusivity,'' with SI units which can be read from comparing sides of the equation: $D\sim \text{m}^2 \text{s}^{-1}$.  This equation has a smoothing effect on the solution $T(t,x)$ as it evolves in time, because any local maximum or minimum in the temperature is flattened: $T_{xx}<0$ implies $T_t<0$, so $T$ decreases near a maximum, while $T_{xx}>0$ implies $T_t>0$, so $T$ increases near a minimum.  All extrema of the temperature diffuse away.

The 2D heat equation, which describes the temperature $T(t,x,y)$ of a planar object, is a closer analog of \eqref{sia}.  Recall that Fourier's law for conduction is the formula $\mathbf{Q} = - \kappa \grad T$ for heat flux $\mathbf{Q}$, where $\kappa$ is conductivity.  We will assume, so as to help the analogy to the SIA, that $\kappa(x,y)$ may vary in space, and we suppose the heat source $f(t,x,y)$ is variable.  Adding conservation of internal energy, we find the equation
\begin{equation}
\rho c T_t = f + \Div (\kappa \grad T). \label{heatearly}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho$ is density and $c$ is specific heat capacity.  Assuming $\rho c$ is constant, define the diffusivity $D=\kappa/(\rho c)$.  Defining $F = f/(\rho c)$, a rescaled source term, \eqref{heatearly} becomes
\begin{equation}
T_t = F + \Div (D\, \grad T). \label{heat}
\end{equation}
This 2D heat equation is similar to the above 1D equation $T_t = D T_{xx}$, but now with variable diffusivity and an additional heat source.

Figure \ref{fig:initialheat} shows a solution of \eqref{heat} in which the initial condition is a localized ``hot spot''.  Solutions of the heat equation always involve the spreading, in all directions, of any local concentration of heat; this is diffusion.

FIXME %\twofigsizes{initialheat}{finalheat}{Left: Initial condition $T(0,x,y)$.   Right: A solution $T(t,x,y)$ of  \eqref{heat}, with $D=1$ and $F=0$, at $t=0.02$.}{2.8in}{2.8in}

The SIA equation \eqref{sia} and the heat equation \eqref{heat} are each diffusive, time-evolving partial differential equations (PDEs).  A side-by-side comparison is appropriate:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc}
\vspace{1mm}
SIA:\, $H$ is ice thickness & \phantom{foo bar} heat: $T$ is temperature\phantom{foo bar}  \\
\vspace{1mm}
	$H_t = M + \Div \left(\Gamma H^{n+2} |\grad h|^{n-1}\, \grad h \right)$  &  $T_t = F + \Div (D\, \grad T)$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vspace{1mm}
Notice that the number of derivatives (one time and two space) and the signs of the various terms are the same.  Surface mass balance $M$ is analogous to heat source $F$.  The analogy suggests that we identify the \emph{SIA diffusivity} as:
\begin{equation}
	D = \Gamma H^{n+2} |\grad h|^{n-1}.  \label{siadiffusivity}
\end{equation}

Diffusion acts quickly where $D$ is large.  SIA diffusivity formula \eqref{siadiffusivity} gives a large value when the ice is thick and steep, because ice flows downhill most-strongly in that case.  Our analogy also explains why the surfaces of ice sheets are smooth, at least once we overlook non-fluid processes like crevassing.

There are, however, conceptual and practical concerns about the \eqref{sia}$\leftrightarrow$\eqref{heat} analogy:
\begin{itemize}
\item The letters ``$H$'' and ``$h$'' generally denote different quantities in \eqref{sia}.  Are we allowed to regard \eqref{sia} as a diffusion anyway, even when the bed is not flat?
\item The diffusivity $D$ in \eqref{siadiffusivity} depends on the solution---both on thickness $H$ and surface slope $|\grad h|$---so equation \eqref{sia} is a nonlinear PDE.  Numerical solutions for nonlinear equations are, at least, more difficult to implement and analyze.
\item The diffusivity $D$ in \eqref{siadiffusivity} goes to zero at margins, where $H\to 0$, and at divides and domes, where $|\grad h|\to 0$.  Thus the solution ($h$ or $H$) is not smooth at these locations, though it is continuous everywhere.  The solution is not smooth even if the bed $b$ and surface mass balance $M$ \emph{are} smooth.  Large numerical errors will arise where the solution is not smooth.
\end{itemize}

Just as important is a physical deficiency of the SIA model, namely
\begin{itemize}
\item Ice flow is not very diffusive when significant longitudinal (membrane) stresses are present, as when ice is floating or sliding, or when the flow is significantly confined by terrain.
\end{itemize}
This concerns a deficiency of the SIA model, not just our analogy to explain it.

All four concerns are areas of active research in mathematical glaciology; see the Notes and References.

The above diffusion analogy suggests that numerical schemes for the heat equation are a good starting point for solving the SIA.  The analogy will lead us to a verified numerical scheme for \eqref{sia}.  We demonstrate only finite difference (FD) schemes, which replace derivatives by difference quotients.

\subsection*{Explicit scheme for the heat equation}  Our simplest scheme approximates the 1D heat equation $T_t=D T_{xx}$ \emph{explicitly}.  It is based on the fact that because $T_t$ and $D T_{xx}$ are equal, these two FD expressions are nearly equal:
\begin{equation}
\frac{T(t+\Delta t,x) - T(t,x)}{\Delta t} \approx D\,\frac{T(t,x+\Delta x) - 2\, T(t,x) + T(t,x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x^2}.  \label{heat1Dapproximated}
\end{equation}
While \eqref{heat1Dapproximated} only approximates the PDE, forcing it to be an equality allows us to determine grid values.  Let $(t_n,x_j)$ denote the points of the time-space grid shown in Figure \ref{fig:timespacegrid}.  It has spacing $\Delta x>0$ and $\Delta t>0$ in the two dimensions.  Denote our approximation of the solution value $T(t_n,x_j)$ by $T_j^n$.  The finite difference scheme is
	$$\frac{T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n}{\Delta t} = D\,\frac{T_{j+1}^n - 2\, T_j^n + T_{j-1}^n}{\Delta x^2}.$$
To get a simplified formula, let $\mu = D \Delta t / (\Delta x)^2$ and solve for $T_j^{n+1}$:
\begin{equation}
  T_j^{n+1} = \mu T_{j+1}^n + (1 - 2 \mu) T_j^n + \mu T_{j-1}^n \label{heat1Dfd}
\end{equation}

FIXME %\onefigsize{timespacegrid}{A grid for a finite difference solution to the 1D heat equation.}{2.0in}

FD scheme \eqref{heat1Dfd} is \emph{explicit} because it directly (explicitly) computes $T_j^{n+1}$ in terms of values at time $t_n$.  Figure \ref{fig:expstencil} (left) shows the ``stencil'' for scheme \eqref{heat1Dfd}: three values at the current time $t_n$ are combined to update one value at the next time $t_{n+1}$.

FIXME %\twofigsizes{expstencil}{exp2dstencil}{Left: Space-time stencil for the explicit scheme \eqref{heat1Dfd} for the 1D heat equation.  Right: Spatial-only stencil for scheme \eqref{heat2dexplicit}.}{2.0in}{2.1in}

The expression $T(t_n,x_j)$ is the value of a heat equation solution at a grid point.  This is generally a different number from $T_j^n$, the value actually computed by a scheme like \eqref{heat1Dfd}.  Because the FD expressions become closer to the derivatives they approximate, we intend that the numbers $T(t_n,x_j)$ and $T_j^n$ become closer together as the grid is made finer ($\Delta t \to 0$ and $\Delta x \to 0$).  That is, we intend our FD scheme to \emph{converge} under \emph{grid refinement}.  Proving that such convergence happens is a main topic in numerical textbooks \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005}.  Even if we expect convergence to happen, this needs checking for the actual computer program which is our implementation (\emph{verification}; Section \ref{sec:exactsolutions}).

How accurate is scheme \eqref{heat1Dfd}?  Our construction tells us that the difference between the scheme \eqref{heat1Dfd} and the PDE it solves is $O(\Delta t + (\Delta x)^2)$.  This difference goes to zero as we refine the grid in space and time, a scheme property called \emph{consistency}.  With care about the smoothness of boundary conditions, and using mathematical facts about the heat equation itself, one can also show that the difference between $T_j^n$ and $T(t_n,x_j)$ is $O(\Delta t + (\Delta x)^2)$, the \emph{convergence rate}.  However, to get convergence the scheme must be \emph{stable}, which we address below.  The main theorem for numerical PDE schemes asserts that ``consistency plus stability implies convergence''.  See the Notes and References.

Instead of pursuing theory, in these notes we emphasize something practical, namely verification using exact solutions.  We will find PDE problems for which we already know an exact solution $T(t,x)$, and then compute differences like $|T_j^n - T(t_n,x_j)|$ and confirm that they go to zero under grid refinement.  This demonstrates directly that our actual implementation converges, not just that it should do so in theory.  A verified code can be confidently applied to real situations.

\subsection*{A first implemented scheme}  Our first Matlab implementation is for the 2D heat equation \eqref{heat} with $D$ constant and $F=0$:
\begin{equation}
T_t = D (T_{xx}+T_{yy}).\label{heat2D}
\end{equation}
We solve this equation on the square $-1 < x < 1$, $-1 < y < 1$.  For a simple example problem we set $T=0$ on the boundary of the square and choose a gaussian initial condition: $T(0,x,y) = \exp(-30 (x^2+y^2))$.

Writing $T_{jk}^n \approx T(t_n,x_j,y_k)$, the 2D explicit scheme is
\begin{equation}
	\frac{T_{jk}^{n+1} - T_{jk}^n}{\Delta t} = D\,\left(\frac{T_{j+1,k}^n - 2\, T_{jk}^n + T_{j-1,k}^n}{\Delta x^2} + \frac{T_{j,k+1}^n - 2\, T_{jk}^n + T_{j,k-1}^n}{\Delta y^2}\right). \label{heat2dexplicit}
\end{equation}
The stencil for the right side of \eqref{heat2dexplicit} is in Figure \ref{fig:expstencil}.  Using Matlab colon notation to implement loops over the spatial variables, our scheme becomes \texttt{heat.m} below.

\subsection*{Stability criteria and adaptive time stepping}  To avoid such instabilities we need to understand the scheme better.  While we have not made an implementation error, we must learn how to choose compatible space and time steps.

Recall the 1D explicit scheme in form \eqref{heat1Dfd}: $T_j^{n+1} = \mu T_{j+1}^n + (1 - 2 \mu) T_j^n + \mu T_{j-1}^n$.  The new value $T_j^{n+1}$ is an average of the old values, in the sense that the coefficients sum to one.  Averaging is stable because averaged wiggles are smaller than the wiggles themselves.

However, the scheme is only an average \emph{if} the coefficients, specifically the middle coefficient $1-2\mu$, are positive.  A linear combination with coefficients which add to one is not an average if some coefficients are negative.  For example, one would not accept 15 as an ``average'' of 5 and 7, but: $15 = -4 \times 5 + 5 \times 7$, and $-4+5=1$.

What consequences follow from requiring the middle coefficient in \eqref{heat1Dfd} to be positive?  We find a \emph{stability criterion}, with these equivalent forms:
\begin{equation}
   1 - 2 \mu \ge 0 \quad \iff \quad \frac{D\Delta t}{\Delta x^2} \le \frac{1}{2} \quad \iff \quad \Delta t \le \frac{\Delta x^2}{2 D}.  \label{stabcrit}
\end{equation}
The third form states the criterion as a limitation on the size of $\Delta t$.  It is a \emph{sufficient} stability condition; it guarantees stability though something weaker might do.

In summary, for a given value of $\Delta x$, shortening the time steps $\Delta t$ until \eqref{stabcrit} holds will make FD scheme \eqref{heat1Dfd} for the 1D heat equation into a stable averaging process.  Applying this same idea to the 2D heat equation \eqref{heat2D} leads to the stability condition $1-2\mu^x-2\mu^y \ge 0$, where $\mu^x = D \Delta t / (\Delta x^2)$ and $\mu^y = D \Delta t / (\Delta y^2)$.  In the cases shown in Figure \ref{fig:stability}, wherein $\Delta x=\Delta y$, this condition requires $D \Delta t /(\Delta x^2) \le 0.25$.  This inequality precisely distinguishes between these stable and unstable results, respectively.

Runs of \texttt{heat.m} are unstable if the time step $\Delta t$ is too large relative to the spacing $\Delta x$.  However, the stability criterion \eqref{stabcrit} can be enforced inside the code, and this is called an \emph{adaptive} implementation.  Changing \texttt{heat.m} in this way yields \texttt{heatadapt.m} (not shown).  Furthermore, such adaptive codes can be stable even if the diffusivity $D$ varies in space or time.  On the other hand, if the diffusivity $D$ is large, or if grid spacings $\Delta x$, $\Delta y$ are small, then adaptive explicit implementations must take many short time steps to assure stability.  Adaptive explicit schemes become inefficient on spatially-refined grids.

\subsection*{Implicit schemes}  \emph{Implicitness} is an alternative stability fix to adaptivity.  For example, the finite difference scheme
\begin{equation}
  \frac{T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n}{\Delta t} = D\,\frac{T_{j+1}^{n+1} - 2\, T_j^{n+1} + T_{j-1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x^2} \label{implicit1D}
\end{equation}
is an $O(\Delta t + (\Delta x)^2)$ implicit scheme for the 1D heat equation $T_t = D T_{xx}$.  This implicit \emph{backward Euler} scheme is stable for any positive time step $\Delta t>0$; it is \emph{unconditionally stable} \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005}.  Another well-known implicit scheme for the heat equation is \emph{Crank-Nicolson}, an unconditionally-stable scheme with smaller error $O((\Delta t)^2 +(\Delta x)^2)$.

Implicit schemes are more difficult to implement because the unknown solution at time step $t_{n+1}$ must be treated as a vector, and a (large) linear or nonlinear system of equations must be formed and solved at each time step.  Though the SIA is a highly-nonlinear diffusion equation, implicit schemes are indeed implementable when appropriate solver tools are used \cite{Bueler2016}.  However, for these notes, in the tradeoff between the easy implementability of adaptive explicit schemes and the improved stability of implicit schemes, we choose the former.

\section{Numerically solving the SIA} \label{sec:numericalsia}

As already noted, we compute the SIA diffusivity $D$ on a staggered grid.  Various schemes for staggered-grid diffusivity have been proposed, but all involve averaging $H$ and differencing $h$ in a ``balanced'' way onto the staggered grid.  In the code \texttt{siaflat.m} below, which only works for a flat bed and zero surface mass balance, we use the Mahaffy \cite{Mahaffy1976} method.  The Mahaffy stencil for computing $D$ is shown in Figure \ref{fig:diffstencil} (right).  Note that this SIA solver calls \texttt{diffstag.m} (above).

For non-flat beds and actual climates, namely when the surface mass balance term $M$ can take either sign, \texttt{siaflat.m} does not suffice.  Its deficiences are corrected in an extended code \texttt{siageneral.m} (not shown), which in fact only alters a few lines.

FIXME %\minput{siaflat}

Addressing ablation ($M<0$) requires a small modification which deserves exposure.  Note that the grounded ice margin occurs in areas of ablation where the SIA thickness solution $H$ goes to zero.  However, the thickness of ice must obviously be greater than or equal to zero everywhere: $H(t,x)\ge 0$.  It follows that geometry-evolving numerical glacier and ice sheet models are solving a \emph{free-boundary problem}.  For an explicit time-stepping scheme it suffices to make the following rule:
\begin{quote}
\emph{If the numerical computation generates an updated thickness which is negative then it is reset to zero:} \, $H_{j,k}^{n+1} \gets \max\{0,H_{j,k}^{n+1}\}$.
\end{quote}
Determining glacier-covered area is a major glaciological purpose for modeling, thus this seemingly-small complication is ubiquitous and unavoidable in explicit time-stepping glacier models if the margin is allowed to move.  Modelers often implement and then overlook the above rule; it violates conservation of mass \cite{Bueler2021conservation}.


\subsection*{Prognostic models}  Consider an explicit, geometry-evolving, isothermal ice sheet model, called a \emph{prognostic} model in glaciology.  Each time step follows this recipe:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item numerically solve an incompressible stress balance, yielding velocity $\mathbf{u}=(u,v,w)$,
    \begin{itemize}
    \item[$\circ$] the ice geometry and the stress boundary conditions determine $\mathbf{u}$
    \item[$\circ$] if the stress balance yields $\mathbf{U}=(u,v)$ then $w$ comes from equation \eqref{incompressible}
    \end{itemize}
  \item decide on a time step $\Delta t$ for \eqref{masscont} based on velocities and/or diffusivities,
  \item from the horizontal velocity $\mathbf{U}=(u,v)$, compute the flux $\bq = \int_b^h \bU dz = \bar{\bU} H$
  \item update mass balance $M=a-s$ and do a time-step of \eqref{masscont} to get $H_t$
  \item update the upper surface elevation and thickness, and repeat.
  \end{itemize}
The last step uses the rules $H \mapsto H + H_t \Delta t$ and $h \mapsto h + H_t \Delta t$, but it also enforces $H\ge 0$ and $h\ge b$ as mentioned above.  Most ice sheet models use the mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont} to describe changes in ice sheet geometry, but \eqref{surfkine} may be used instead.

The above ``standard explicit ice sheet model'' has many variations.  Some glaciological questions are answered just by solving the stress balance for the velocity, so the geometry does not evolve.  Sometimes the goal is the steady-state configuration of the glacier, both geometry and velocity, in which case the constraint $H\ge 0$ should be regarded as a part of the continuum formulation and the solver \cite{Bueler2016,JouvetBueler2012}.

Other processes are often simulated at each time step, such as the conservation of energy within the ice, or subglacial and supraglacial processes.  Understanding the diverse time scales associated to these processes is always an important step in designing a model which extends our ``introductory'' isothermal SIA model.

It may seem that when using SIA equation \eqref{sia} one bypasses the computation of the ice velocity.  That is because the mass continuity equation can be written as a diffusion, with $\bq=-D\nabla h$ for the flux.  Fast flow in ice sheets is associated with sliding and floating ice, however, and for these flows the ice geometry evolution is not a diffusion.  A complete ice flow model will be neither diffusive nor wave-like (hyperbolic), but the general formula $\bq = \bar{\bU} H$ will apply everywhere.  The ice velocity is always related in some way to the local surface gradient, but usually one must solve membrane-stress-balancing equations.  Solving the stress balance for the velocity field is both a generally-nontrivial and obligatory step in a numerical ice flow model.  The next section explores one important case.


\section{Exact solutions and verification} \label{sec:exactsolutions}

Program \texttt{siaflat.m} calls \texttt{diffstag.m}, so we already have a complicated code.  How can we make sure that such an implemented numerical scheme is correct?  Here are three proposed techniques in the modeling community:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item don't make any mistakes, or
  \item compare your numerical results with results from other researchers, and hope the outliers are in error, or
  \item compare your numerical results to an exact solution.   \end{enumerate}
The first two approaches, \emph{infallibility} and \emph{intercomparison} respectively, should be less common than they are.  The last one, preferred by the CFD community generally \cite{Wesseling2001}, is \emph{verification}.  It is a simple idea:  A computer code should be tested in cases where we know the right answer.  To do this we must return to the PDE itself.  We must seek-out useful exact solutions.

\subsection*{Exact solutions of heat equation}  Again consider the 1D heat equation $T_t = D T_{xx}$ with constant $D$.  Many exact solutions are known, but we will use the time-dependent \emph{Green's function}, circa 1830, also known as the \emph{heat kernel}.  It starts at time $t=0$ with a delta function $T(0,x)=\delta(x)$ of heat at the origin.  Then it spreads out over time, solving the equation on $-\infty<x<\infty$ and for $t>0$.  We calculate it by a method which generalizes to the SIA.

This Green's function is self-similar over time, in the sense that it changes shape \emph{only} by shrinking the output (vertical) axis and lengthening the input (horizontal) axis (Figure \ref{fig:heatscaling}).  These scalings are related to each other by conservation of energy.  Similarity variables for this solution involve multiplying the input and output of an invariant shape $\phi(s)$ by the same power of $t$:
\begin{equation}
s \stackrel{\text{\emph{input scaling}}}{\phantom{\Big|}=\phantom{\Big|}} t^{-1/2} x, \qquad\qquad T(t,x) \stackrel{\text{\emph{output scaling}}}{\phantom{\Big|}=\phantom{\Big|}} t^{-1/2} \phi(s).  \label{heatscalings}
\end{equation}
One may use $T_t = D T_{xx}$ to show that $\phi(s) = (4 \pi D)^{-1/2}\, e^{-s^2/(4D)}$, and thus
    $$T(t,x) = (4 \pi D t)^{-1/2}\, e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}.$$

To verify a numerical solver for the 1D heat equation using this exact solution we may evaluate this exact solution at $t_0>0$ to provide initial values.  At a later time $t_1>t_0$ the numerical solution should be close to the exact solution $T(t_1,x)$ (see the Exercises).

\subsection*{Halfar's similarity solution to the SIA}  Now we jump to the year 1981 in which Peter Halfar \cite{Halfar1981} published the corresponding similarity solution of the SIA, at least for flat bed and zero surface mass balance.  For the 2D SIA model \eqref{sia} using a Glen exponent $n=3$ this solution \cite{Halfar1983} has scalings similar to \eqref{heatscalings} above:
\begin{equation}
s = t^{-1/18} r, \qquad \qquad H(t,r)=t^{-1/9} \phi(s), \label{halfarscalings}
\end{equation}
where $r=(x^2+y^2)^{1/2}$ is the distance from the origin.  These scalings are related to each other by the constancy of total mass/volume, because no mass is gained or lost through the surface (see the Exercises). Scalings \eqref{halfarscalings} imply that, differently from heat, the diffusion of ice slows down dramatically as the shape flattens out.  That is, the powers $t^{-1/9}$ and $t^{-1/18}$ change very slowly for large times $t$.

FIXME %\onefigsize{siascaling}{Radial sections of Halfar's solution \eqref{halfar} of \eqref{sia}, shown on $H$ (m) versus $r$ (km) axes at $t=1,10,100,1000,10000$ years.}{5.5in}

The Halfar solution formula is remarkably simple given all that it accomplishes:
\begin{equation}
H(t,r) = H_0 \left(\frac{t_0}{t}\right)^{1/9} \left[1 - \left(\left(\frac{t_0}{t}\right)^{1/18} \frac{r}{R_0}\right)^{4/3}\right]^{3/7}. \label{halfar}
\end{equation}
Here the characteristic time $t_0 = (18 \Gamma)^{-1} (7/4)^3 R_0^4 H_0^{-7}$ depends on the radius $R_0$ and the center height $H_0$.  In Figure \ref{fig:siascaling} we see that for times significantly greater than $t_0$ ($t/t_0 \gg 1$) the solution changes very slowly.  For example, the change between years $1$ and $100$ is larger than that between years $1000$ and $10000$.

\subsection*{Using Halfar's solution}  Formula \eqref{halfar} is simple to use for verifying time-dependent SIA models.  The code \texttt{verifysia.m} (not shown) takes as input the number of grid points in each ($x,y$) direction.  It uses the Halfar solution at 200 a as the initial condition, does a numerical run of \texttt{siaflat.m} to a final time 20000 a, and then compares to the Halfar formula at that time.  It computes the thickness \emph{numerical error}, the absolute difference between the numerical and exact thicknesses at the final time:
\small
\begin{verbatim}
>> verifysia(20)
average thickness error  = 22.310
>> verifysia(40)
average thickness error  = 9.459
>> verifysia(80)
average thickness error  = 2.771
>> verifysia(160)
average thickness error  = 1.085
\end{verbatim}
\normalsize

The average numerical thickness error decreases with increasing grid resolution, as expected for a correctly-implemented code.  What is less obvious is that almost any numerical implementation mistake will break such convergence.  That is, the reported errors will not shrink if there is any meaningful bug (see the Exercises).  The fragility of verification is its essential benefit!  One must implement the model correctly, not just generate good-looking results, to get convergence as above.

The Halfar solution has also been used for modelling real ice masses.  Nye and others \cite{Nyeetal2000} used it to compare the long-time consequences of different flow laws for the south polar cap on Mars.  In particular, they evaluated $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ice softness parameters by comparing the long-time behavior of the corresponding Halfar solutions to the observed polar cap properties.  Their conclusions:
  \begin{quote}
  \dots none of the three possible [$\text{CO}_2$] flow laws will allow a 3000-m cap, the thickness suggested by stereogrammetry, to survive for $10^7$ years, indicating that the south polar ice cap is probably not composed of pure $\text{CO}_2$ ice [but rather] water ice, \dots
  \end{quote}
This theoretical result has since been confirmed by sampling the surface of Mars.

Are exact solutions like Halfar's always available when needed?  No, of course not.  However, many ice flow models have exact solutions which are relevant to verification; see the Notes and References.  For example, we will use an exact solution for ice shelves in a later section.  An apparent absence of exact solutions to a given model may actually show that not enough thought has gone into the continuum model itself.

\subsection*{A test of robustness}  Verification is an ideal way to test a code, but another worthwhile test is for ``robustness'' in unusual or difficult input cases.  One asks: Does the model break?  Here one may not have a precise expectation of what the code \emph{should} do, but it should not produce obviously-unreasonable outputs.

The robustness test run by \texttt{roughice.m} (not shown) demonstrates that \texttt{siaflat.m} can handle an ice sheet with extraordinarily-large driving stresses.  Recall that the glaciological driving stress $\tau_d = - \rho g H \grad h$ appears in the SIA model as the shear stress $(\tau_{13},\tau_{23})$ at the base of the ice.  The driving stress is largest where the ice is thick and the surface slope $|\nabla h|$ is steep.  In our test we give \texttt{siaflat.m} a randomly-generated initial ice sheet which has huge driving stresses.  It is thick---average of 3000 m---with huge surface slopes because of random surface elevation values.  An initial shape is shown in Figure \ref{fig:roughinitial} (left).  During a run of just 50 model years on a 17 km grid, with time step determined adaptively from \eqref{stabcrit}, the maximum diffusivity $D$ decreases as the surface both lowers and becomes smooth through flow.  The time-step consequently increases, in this case by three orders of magnitude from about 0.0002 years to 0.2 years.  The maximum value of the driving stress decreases from $57$ bar ($= 5.7\times 10^6$ Pa) to $3.6$ bar.  At the end of the run the ice cap has the shape shown in Figure \ref{fig:roughinitial} (right).

The final shape in Figure 12 is close to a Halfar solution, illustrating what he proved \cite{Halfar1981,Halfar1983}, namely that \emph{all} solutions of the zero-mass-balance SIA on a flat bed asymptotically approach his solution.  The Halfar solution is attracting and thus worth knowing.


\section{Summary} \label{sec:summary}

These brief notes give an incomplete view of numerical ice flow models for glaciers and ice sheets.  As a meager conclusion here are four high-level views of such models:
\begin{itemize}
\item The mass continuity equation, a conservation equation in the map-plane, describes ice thickness changes.  In most ice sheet models it is the component which describes how the geometry evolves.  The numerical approach to this equation depends on the form of the stress balance which supplies the ice velocity, but, since it is a conservation equation, it is common to evaluate the ice flux on the staggered grid (finite difference thinking) or on cell boundaries (finite volume thinking).
\item The mass continuity equation has diffusive character (SIA-like) at larger spatial scales and in areas with significant basal resistance, but it is \emph{not} very diffusive for membrane-stress dominated areas.  Ice streams and shelves are often modeled by the membrane-stress-resolving SSA.
\item The SIA stress balance is exceptional because it contains no horizontal derivatives of the velocities.  It follows that in the SIA the velocity can be found simply by vertical integration of geometric quantities (e.g.~the driving stress).  No other stress balance is like this.
\item Membrane-stress-resolving balance equations, such as the SSA, Blatter-Pattyn, hydrostatic, and Stokes models, all determine horizontal velocity from solving spatial, elliptic-like PDEs using the ice geometry and stress boundary conditions \cite{BuelerBrown2009}.  Because of the Glen law these PDEs are nonlinear, so iteration is necessary.  At each iteration a sparse matrix ``inner'' problem is typically solved using a linear-algebra solver package, e.g.~a high-performance library like PETSc \cite{Bueler2021}.
\end{itemize}
