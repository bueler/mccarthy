\section{Introduction}  \label{sec:intro}

\subsection*{Purpose}  The greatest importance of mathematical and numerical models to science, and glaciology in particular, is \emph{introspection}.  In building and using a model you are asking yourself: When I combine my imperfect and incomplete understanding of glacier processes into this model, does it behave as I expect?  A numerical simulation can only report the consequences of your equations, inadequacies and all, but the approximation can show how processes interact to give overall behavior, and it can sometimes demonstrate flaws in your understanding of those processes.  In any case, while all computer programs produce numbers, we want numbers that closely-approximate our intended continuum equations.  Therefore the differential equations, not just the Python programs, will be a primary focus of these notes.

\subsection*{Scope}  These notes are deliberately limited in scope:
  \begin{quote}\emph{surface kinematics and shallow approximations of ice flow.}\end{quote}
Defining these technical terms is a big part of our agenda.  This scope includes a surface kinematical equation, and the equations of the shallow ice approximation.

For these topics there is a constructive approach:
  \begin{quote}\emph{understandable and short Python codes that do particular jobs.}\end{quote}

In truth, we start the presentation with the non-shallow Glen-Stokes model.  There is a Firedrake-based \cite{Rathgeberetal2016} Stokes solver in \texttt{py/stokes/}; its documentation \texttt{doc.pdf} is a technical appendix to these notes.  However, these notes do not explore the numerical solutions of a Stokes model.

\section{Ice flow equations}  \label{sec:continuum}

\subsection*{Glacier ice is a weird fluid}  Ice in glaciers behaves as a viscous fluid \cite{GreveBlatter2009}, and we describe its motion by a velocity field $\mathbf{u}$.  It is incompressible.  If the ice were faster-moving than it is in reality, and linearly-viscous like liquid water, then we would use the Navier-Stokes partial differential equations as the model for this typical fluid \cite{Wesseling2001}:
\begin{align}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} &= 0 &&\text{\emph{incompressibility}} \label{incompressible} \\
\rho \left(\mathbf{u}_t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla \mathbf{u}\right) &= \nabla \cdot (\nu \nabla \mathbf{u}) - \nabla p + \rho \mathbf{g} &&\text{\emph{stress balance}} \label{navierstokes}
\end{align}

Besides the velocity $\bu$, the other symbols in these equations are the ice density $\rho$, the viscosity $\nu$, the pressure $p$, and the acceleration of gravity $\bg$.  The velocity field $\bu$ is divergence-free (equation \eqref{incompressible}); thus no fluid is created or lost within the domain, which is one aspect of mass conservation.  Equation \eqref{navierstokes} is a form of ``$ma=F$'', Newton's second law.  The left side of \eqref{navierstokes} has the acceleration following the fluid, $\mathbf{u}_t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla \mathbf{u}$, while the right-hand side is the total force per unit volume, the sum of viscous, pressure, and gravity ($\rho \mathbf{g} = \left<0,0,-\rho g\right>$) forces, respectively.  Using $\sigma$ to denote the Cauchy stress tensor, the right side can be written $\nabla \cdot \sigma + \rho \mathbf{g}$.

The space of solutions to \eqref{incompressible} and \eqref{navierstokes} is rich.  It includes turbulent flows, which we will not need here.  However, reading a book-length introduction to fluids such as \cite{Acheson1990}, or their numerical solution (\emph{computational fluid dynamics}; e.g.~\cite{Wesseling2001}), may not even be helpful for glacier modeling.

One might ask: is glacier modeling really computational fluid dynamics at all?  The answer is \emph{yes}.  Glacier ice flow is a large-scale, climate-relevant fluid problem, comparable to ocean circulation, for example.  However, phenomena which make ocean flows exciting, including turbulence, convection, Coriolis force, and density stratification, are all absent.  What could be interesting about the slow flow of ice?  How does it work, anyway?

\subsection*{Glen-Stokes equations}  We have said that glacier ice is a ``slow'' fluid.  In terms of equation \eqref{navierstokes}, this means that $\rho \left(\mathbf{u}_t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla \mathbf{u}\right) \approx 0$, the forces (stresses) of inertia are negligible, so Newton's second law becomes a \emph{stress balance}.  Ice is also a shear-thinning fluid, with \emph{non-Newtonian} behavior; larger strain rates imply smaller viscosity.  The nonlinear viscosity $\nu$ is thus not constant, and an empirically-based flow law is needed.  Combining these few ideas gives the canonical ice flow model:
\begin{align}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} &= 0 &&\text{\emph{incompressibility}} \label{incompressibleagain} \\
- \nabla \cdot \tau + \nabla p &= \rho \mathbf{g} &&\text{\emph{stress balance}} \label{forcebalance} \\
D\bu &= A |\tau|^{n-1} \tau &&\text{\emph{Glen-Steinemann-Nye flow law}} \label{flowlaw}
\end{align}
Note that $\rho>0$ and $\bg$ are constant, and the \emph{ice softness} $A>0$ is constant for the purposes of our simplified treatment, and $n$ is often taken to be three, but the other fields depend on $t,x,y,z$.

Equations \eqref{incompressibleagain}--\eqref{flowlaw} are commonly called the \emph{Stokes model} in glaciology.  Regarding the details, note that $|\tau|^2 = (1/2) \tau_{ij} \tau_{ij}$, using the summation convention, which defines the \emph{effective deviatoric stress} $|\tau|$.  By definition $D\bu = \frac{1}{2}(\grad \bu + \grad \bu^\top)$, thus $(D\bu)_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left((u_i)_{x_j}+(u_j)_{x_i}\right)$ in components.  In \eqref{flowlaw} the deviatoric stress $\tau$ and strain rate $D\bu$ tensors are proportional, with a scalar power-law coefficient $A|\tau|^{n-1}$; this was Nye's contribution to the flow law form.  Since $D\bu$ is symmetric, it follows from \eqref{incompressibleagain} and \eqref{flowlaw} that both tensors $\tau$, $D\bu$ have trace zero.

There are many aspects of the Stokes model one might learn.  For example, the many possible solutions of \eqref{forcebalance}--\eqref{flowlaw} are constrained by incompressibility \eqref{incompressibleagain}, and in this way of thinking the pressure $p$ serves as a Lagrange multiplier for this constraint.

However, note something simple: equations \eqref{incompressibleagain}--\eqref{flowlaw} do not contain any time derivatives!  This implies that the combined effect of glacier geometry, boundary stresses, and gravity will determine the solution velocity and stress fields ($\bu$, $p$, $\tau$) at each instant \cite{JouvetRappaz2011}.  Velocity is therefore a \emph{diagnostic} variable: ice flow codes need no stored velocity to restart, unlike in a Navier-Stokes simulation.  The equations apply at every time, but as the glacier interacts with the climate its geometry and basal stresses are changing, and thus the Stokes solution fields are also changing.  In other words, though these equations have no time derivatives, the modeled glacier is \emph{not} generally in steady state.

\subsection*{Planar Stokes model}  Consider the $x,z$-coordinates \emph{planar} (or \emph{flow line}) and $n=3$ case.  The out-of-plane velocity component in equations \eqref{incompressibleagain}--\eqref{flowlaw} is zero, i.e.~$v=0$, and derivatives with respect to $y$ are also zero:
\begin{align}
u_x + w_z &= 0 &&\text{\emph{incompressibility}} \label{incompressiblexz} \\
- \tau_{11,x} - \tau_{13,z} + p_x &= 0 &&\text{\emph{stress balance} ($x$)} \label{stokespx} \\
- \tau_{13,x} + \tau_{11,z} + p_z &= - \rho g &&\text{\emph{stress balance} ($z$)} \label{stokespz} \\
u_x &= A \tau^2 \tau_{11} &&\text{\emph{flow law (diagonal)}}  \label{forceflowx} \\
u_z + w _x &= 2 A \tau^2 \tau_{13} &&\text{\emph{flow law (off-diagonal)}} \label{forceflowz}
\end{align}

Here $\tau_{13}$ is a shear stress while $\tau_{11},\tau_{33}$ are deviatoric longitudinal stresses.  In this planar case it follows that $\tau_{33}=-\tau_{11}$, by incompressibility \eqref{incompressiblexz} and the flow law \eqref{forceflowx}--\eqref{forceflowz}, which explains why we can write \eqref{stokespz} without $\tau_{33}$.  Also, a short calculation shows $\tau^2 = \tau_{11}^2+\tau_{13}^2$.

Observe that equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}--\eqref{forceflowz} form a system of 5 nonlinear equations in 5 unknown scalar fields ($u,w,p,\tau_{11},\tau_{13}$).

\subsection*{Slab-on-a-slope solution}  However, these 5 equations are still complicated and difficult to understand!  Before jumping into numerical solution methods we consider a simplified situation, a uniform slab of ice on an incline.  Here we can exactly solve the Stokes equations.  The same calculation motivates a later shallow model.

\onefig{slab}{Rotated axes for the slab-on-a-slope calculation.}

We rotate our coordinates only for this calculation.  The $x,z$ axes shown in Figure \ref{fig:slab} are rotated clockwise at angle $\alpha>0$ so that the gravity vector now has components $\mathbf{g} = (g \sin\alpha,- g \cos \alpha)$.  Equations \eqref{stokespx} and \eqref{stokespz} have the new form
\begin{align}
- \tau_{11,x} - \tau_{13,z} + p_x &= \rho g \sin\alpha, \label{stokespxrot} \\
- \tau_{13,x} + \tau_{11,z} + p_z &= - \rho g \cos\alpha. \label{stokespzrot}
\end{align}

For this uniform slab we assume further that there is no variation with $x$. Then the system of 5 equations simplifies greatly:
\begin{equation}
w_z = 0, \quad \tau_{11} = 0, \quad u_z = 2 A \tau^2 \tau_{13}, \quad \tau_{13,z} = - \rho g \sin\alpha, \quad p_z = - \rho g \cos\alpha. \label{stokesslab}
\end{equation}
Each unknown is now a function of $z$ only.  Boundary conditions come from assuming that the ice is not crossing the bed and that the stress-free ice surface is moving parallel to the $x$-axis.  Thus: $w(0)=0$, $p(H)=0$, $\tau_{13}(H)=0$.  We already have $\tau_{11}(z)=0$, and by integrating 3 equations in \eqref{stokesslab} with respect to $z$, we get $w(z)=0$, $p(z) = (\rho g \cos\alpha) (H-z)$, and $\tau_{13}(z) = (\rho g \sin\alpha) (H-z)$.  Note that $H-z$ is the depth below the ice surface; pressure and shear stress are proportional to depth.  Integrating the remaining equation yields the horizontal velocity function, using notation $u(0)=u_0$:
\begin{align}
u(z) &= u_0 + \frac{1}{2} A (\rho g \sin\alpha)^3  \left(H^4 - (H-z)^4\right)  \label{uslab}
\end{align}

Do we believe formula \eqref{uslab}, which makes a specific prediction about the profile of flow in a glacier?  Figure \ref{fig:slabvel} compares observations of a mountain glacier, showing that this model does a credible job of capturing deformation flow in this case.  However, the basal velocity $u_0=u(0)$ is generally unknown.  For simplicity we will assume in these notes that there is no sliding ($u_0=0$), for example because the ice is frozen to the bed.  (However, effective models for glacier sliding are available \cite{BuelerBrown2009}.)  In Section \ref{sec:sia} we will convert the severe simplifications of this slab-on-a-slope model into the \emph{shallow ice approximation}.

\twofigsizes{slabvel}{athabasca-deform}{Left:  Velocity from formula \eqref{uslab}.  Right:  Measured velocity in a glacier (Athabasca Glacier \cite{SavagePaterson1963}).}{2.0in}{1.8in}

\subsection*{Forms of the flow law}  Returning to the general case, flow law \eqref{flowlaw} has another important form.  Let us consider the $n=3$ case for simplicity.  Recall that $|\tau|^2 = (1/2) \tau_{ij} \tau_{ij}$, and define $|D\bu|^2 = (1/2) (D\bu)_{ij} (D\bu)_{ij}$; the scalars $|\tau|$ and $|D\bu|$ are tensor \emph{norms}.  From \eqref{flowlaw} we get $|D\bu| = A |\tau|^3$.  Thus $|\tau| = A^{-1/3} |D\bu|^{1/3}$, and \eqref{flowlaw} can be rewritten
\begin{equation}
\tau = 2 \nu\, D\bu  \qquad \text{\emph{viscosity form of a flow law}} \label{viscosityflowlaw}
\end{equation}
where the (nonlinear) \emph{effective viscosity} $\nu$ has this form:
\begin{equation}
\nu = \frac{1}{2} A^{-1/3} |D\bu|^{-2/3}. \label{viscositypowerlaw}
\end{equation}

Equations \eqref{viscosityflowlaw} and \eqref{viscositypowerlaw} eliminate the stress tensor $\tau$ from the stress balance \eqref{forcebalance}, so that the Stokes formulas only need velocity $\bu$, pressure $p$, and strain rates $D\bu$ as major variables.  Often $B = A^{-1/3}$ is called the \emph{ice hardness}.

\subsection*{Hydrostatic and Blatter-Pattyn approximations}  A relatively-mild simplification of the Glen-Stokes equations comes from assuming that horizontal variation in the vertical shear stress is small compared to the other terms.  This removes the single term $\tau_{13,x}$ from the $z$-component of the (planar) stress balance \eqref{stokespz}, giving
\begin{equation}
\tau_{11,z} + p_z = - \rho g. \label{hydrostaticpz}
\end{equation}
Because $\sigma_{ij} = \tau_{ij} - p \delta_{ij}$, so that $\tau_{11}+p = - \tau_{33}+p = - \sigma_{33}$, equation \eqref{hydrostaticpz} says that the vertical normal stress $\sigma_{33}$ is linear in depth, that is, it is \emph{hydrostatic}.\footnote{The pressure $p$ itself is not generally hydrostatic in this model.}  Also taking a surface value of $\sigma_{33}=0$ we get
\begin{equation}
\tau_{11}+p = \rho g (s-z), \label{hydrostaticitself}
\end{equation}
where $z=s(t,x)$ is the \emph{surface elevation}.

Equation \eqref{hydrostaticitself} allows us to eliminate the pressure $p$ from the model equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}--\eqref{forceflowz}.  Taking the $x$-derivative of \eqref{hydrostaticitself}, substituting into \eqref{stokespx} to eliminate $p_x$, and using the viscosity form \eqref{viscosityflowlaw} leads to this equation \cite{GreveBlatter2009}:
\begin{equation}
\left(4 \nu u_x\right)_x + \left(\nu (u_z+w_x)\right)_z = \rho g s_x \qquad\text{\emph{hydrostatic stress balance}} \label{stresshydrostatic}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{incompressiblexz}, \eqref{viscositypowerlaw}, \eqref{stresshydrostatic}, plus appropriate boundary conditions, determine $u$ and $w$, without the simultaneous need to solve for $p$.  (The remaining unknowns can be determined by differentiation and/or integration in $z$.)  However, while $p$ is gone, \eqref{stresshydrostatic} remains non-trivially coupled to incompressibility \eqref{incompressiblexz} through the vertical velocity $w$.

We get the even-simpler Blatter-Pattyn model by dropping $w_x$ from equation \eqref{stresshydrostatic}:
\begin{equation}
\left(4 \nu u_x\right)_x + \left(\nu u_z\right)_z = \rho g s_x \qquad\text{\emph{Blatter-Pattyn stress balance}} \label{stressblatter}
\end{equation}
Now solving \eqref{viscositypowerlaw}, \eqref{stressblatter} as a boundary value problem yields the scalar horizontal velocity $u$.  We can then recover $w$ using integration in $z$ on incompressibility \eqref{incompressiblexz}.

Because it has a form similar to the well-understood Poisson equation $u_{xx} + u_{zz} = f$ \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005}, the process of solving equation \eqref{stressblatter} may be more familiar than \eqref{stresshydrostatic}.  However, the omission of $w_x$ from the stress balance, a mechanism which smooths the surface of a glacier once the model is made time-dependent (next!), is significant.  The hydrostatic and Blatter-Pattyn stress balances give different predictions.


\section{Surface kinematical equation (evolution of glaciers)}   \label{sec:masscont}

\subsection*{Time-dependent ice domain}  The Glen-Stokes stress balance model for ice flow, and its approximations, are incomplete as models for glacier behavior in a very important sense.  They describe the ice flow at every instant but they give no direct clue about how glaciers \emph{change}.  The stress balance equations contain no time derivatives, and they can not directly ``see'' the climate around the glacier,\footnote{In fact the stress balance ``sees'' basal stress as a boundary condition, and this is sometimes regarded as a climate input, but via highly-nontrivial additional models.} other than gravity.

On the other hand, the \emph{domain} on which the stress balance equations are solved evolves in time.  We need notation for this time-dependent geometry.  Let $s(t,x,y)$ be the ice surface elevation in 3D; the mathematical surface itself is $z=s(t,x,y)$.  We assume for now that the glacier bed elevation $b(x,y)$ is time-independent.\footnote{Erosion and earth deformation (e.g.~glacial isostasy) processes would make the bed elevation time-dependent, in which case a \emph{basal kinematical equation} \eqref{bke} is also needed.}  Our preferred stress balance differential equations are then solved on the 3D set
\begin{equation}
\left\{(x,y,z)\,:\,b(x,y) < z < s(t,x,y)\right\}, \label{domain}
\end{equation}
the changing set of points between the surface and the bed.  In glacier equations it is primarily the ice domain \eqref{domain} that evolves in time, while the stress balance equations are instantaneous truths which hold on this evolving domain.

The figure on the cover page shows notation in the planar (2D) case: $z=s(t,x)$, $z=b(x)$, and $\left\{(x,z)\,:\,b(x) < z < s(t,x)\right\}$ (labeled \textsf{ice} in the figure).

\subsection*{Surface kinematics}  The upper surface evolves in part through the \emph{surface mass balance} (SMB) rate, which is denoted $a(t,x,y)$ or $a(t,x)$ and is also known as the \emph{climatic} mass balance \cite{Cogleyetal2011}.  (``SMB'' is one of a few abbreviations used in these notes; see Table \ref{tab:abbrev}.)  By definition this is the balance between accumulating snow and the comparatively-rapid\footnote{In these notes, and in much of the glacier literature, the velocity of liquid runoff is assumed to be so much larger than the ice flow velocity that the runoff acts instantaneously.} loss of liquid water, by melt and runoff of the snow, firn, or ice, from the upper surface.  Our elevations are in meters, and time is in seconds, so the SMB function $a$ here is in ice-equivalent meters per second.

These notes only consider simulations of land-based glaciers, and we omit models of basal melt, but such melt would contribute to an additional \emph{basal} mass balance rate.  It can be of either sign because of \emph{freeze-on}, e.g.~in floating ice shelves.

The upper surface evolves according to the \emph{surface kinematical equation} (SKE; \cite{SchoofHewitt2013}), which in the planar case is the equation
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} = a + w -u \frac{\partial s}{\partial x}. \label{skeplanar}
\end{equation}

Regarding the notation, the planar ice velocity is $\bu=\left<u,w\right>$, and the variables $u,w$ in \eqref{skeplanar} are these component values at the upper surface, namely $u=u(t,x,s(t,x))$ and $w=w(t,x,s(t,x))$, i.e.~at $z=s(t,x)$.

Equation \eqref{skeplanar} says that the ice surface moves up and down at the rate $\partial s/\partial t$ according to three contributions, the SMB $a$, the vertical ice velocity $w$, and the product of horizontal ice velocity $u$ with the surface slope $\partial s/\partial x$.  Dimensionally, at least, this is correct; $\partial s/\partial x$ is unitless, and all terms in \eqref{skeplanar} are in meters per second.

Vector notation is convenient when extending \eqref{skeplanar} to 3D, so that the surface elevation function $s(t,x,y)$ depends on two horizontal variables.  Let
\begin{equation}
\bn_s = \Big<-\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}, -\frac{\partial s}{\partial y}, \,1\Big> \label{normal}
\end{equation}
be an upward-pointing normal vector on the surface.  Then the SKE is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} - \bu \cdot \bn_s = a  \label{ske}
\end{equation}
where $\bu=\left<u,v,w\right>$ (Table \ref{tab:notation}) is evaluated at the surface.  With all terms involving the unknown surface elevation $s$ on the left, form \eqref{ske} suggests we might think of the climate $a$ as given data, and we might try to solve the SKE for $s$, which is exactly what we are about to do.

The SKE is described in the glacier literature as a mere boundary condition \cite{GreveBlatter2009,SchoofHewitt2013}, but it is actually the most important statement of mass conservation in a glacier model.  It can be derived from conservation by a standard ``pillbox'' calculus argument \cite{Aschwandenetal2012}.

\subsection*{First numerical glacier model}  Regarding equation \eqref{skeplanar} or \eqref{ske} one might say:
\begin{center}
\emph{by numerically solving this equation for $s$, one has a model for a glacier.}
\end{center}
Perhaps it is better to say that the SKE is \emph{correct} in a physical sense and can be \emph{understood} as a glacier model.  We will demonstrate it as a minimal model.

The plan is to numerically solve the planar SKE \eqref{skeplanar} for the surface elevation $s(t,x)$.  To do this we make the bold assumptions that the SMB function $a(t,x)$, \emph{and} the surface values of the velocity components, denoted $u(t,x)$ and $w(t,x)$, are given by other agents.  In practice the SMB could be the output of a sub-model, such as a mass/energy process model coupled to atmosphere physics and data \cite{vanderVeen2013}.  The surface velocity components $u,w$ would likewise be the output of a stress balance sub-model (Sections \ref{sec:continuum} and \ref{sec:sia}).  However, the stress balance sub-model depends on the surface elevation $s$ itself, which determines the ice domain geometry,\footnote{The SMB could also depend on $s$, another form of coupling.} and so in fact the whole problem is non-trivially \emph{coupled}, but we must start somewhere.

This first numerical model is in a short Python program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface.py}{\texttt{py/surface.py}}.  (See the Download part of Section \ref{sec:intro}.)  It generates an approximation to the surface elevation $s(t,x)$, over intervals of space $0<x<L$ and time $0<t<T$, according to \eqref{skeplanar}, by an \emph{explicit} time-dependent numerical method.  We'll use this first program to consider questions of accuracy and stability, and practicalities like verification.

\subsection*{Finite differences}  The program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface.py}{\texttt{py/surface.py}} is based on \emph{finite difference} (FD) approximations \cite{LeVeque2007, MortonMayers2005}, which need an introduction on their own.

From calculus, Taylor's theorem says that for a smooth function $f(x)$ and a real number $\Delta$, we have an expansion:
\begin{equation}
f(x+\Delta) = f(x) + f'(x) \Delta + \frac{1}{2} f''(x) \Delta^2 + \frac{1}{3!} f'''(x) \Delta^3 + \dots  \label{taylor}
\end{equation}
(The precise theorem uses a finite sum and estimates the remainder, but a loose version will suffice here.)  One may replace ``$\Delta$'' by its negative, or other multiples, to get related forms such as $f(x-\Delta) = f(x) - f'(x) \Delta + \frac{1}{2} f''(x) \Delta^2 - \dots$  FD numerical models are constructed by combining such expressions to approximate the derivatives in the continuum equations.  The resulting algebraic equations relate unknown gridded values via mere arithmetic, which digital computers can do well.

As a simple example, subtract $f(x)$ from both sides of expansion \eqref{taylor}, then divide by $\Delta$, to find an approximation of the first derivative,
	$$\frac{f(x+\Delta) - f(x)}{\Delta} \approx f'(x).$$
This is usually written to emphasize the size of the remaining terms in the expansion, or more precisely the size of the remainder in Taylor's theorem:
	$$f'(x) = \frac{f(x+\Delta) - f(x)}{\Delta} + O(\Delta).$$
Here $O(\Delta)$ denotes a quantity with magnitude smaller than a fixed multiple of $\Delta$, asymptotically as $\Delta \to 0$.

To apply this FD approach to a \emph{partial} differential equation (PDE) like SKE \eqref{skeplanar}, let us choose a time step $\Delta t>0$ and a grid spacing $\Delta x>0$.  Apply Taylor expansions in each variable to approximate the partial derivatives in the equation:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}(t,x) &= \frac{s(t+\Delta t,x) - s(t,x)}{\Delta t} + O(\Delta t), \label{fd:partials} \\
\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(t,x) &= \frac{s(t,x+\Delta x) - s(t,x-\Delta x)}{2\Delta x} + O((\Delta x)^2). \notag
\end{align}
The first formula is called a \emph{forward difference} and the second a \emph{centered difference}.

Our numerical solution will use a spatial grid $\{x_j\}$, with equal spacing $\Delta x$ and an integer index $j$.  It will use a temporal grid $\{t_n\}$ with (possibly unequal) spacing $\Delta t$.  We seek a new surface elevation $s(t_{n+1},x_j)$ from some current values $s(t_n,x_{\bullet})$.

We should distinguish between the continuum solution value $s(t_n,x_j)$, by evaluating the exact solution of the continuum SKE \eqref{skeplanar} at a grid point, and our numerical approximation of it at the same point, now denoted $s_j^n$.  Conceptually, $s_j^n \approx s(t_n,x_j)$, but the values $s_j^n$ are the ones we actually get by computation.  Finally, we will declutter the grid notation by writing $s_j$ for $s_j^n$ and $\snew_j$ for $s_j^{n+1}$.

With all these conventions, consider replacements \eqref{fd:partials}, the FD approximations
    $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} \approx \frac{\snew_j - s_j}{\Delta t}, \qquad \frac{\partial s}{\partial x} \approx \frac{s_{j+1} - s_{j-1}}{2\Delta x}.$$
They convert the SKE \eqref{skeplanar} into a discrete and algebraic equation:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\snew_j - s_j}{\Delta t} = a_j + w_j - u_j \frac{s_{j+1} - s_{j-1}}{2\Delta x}. \end{equation*}
This is intended to be a line of code which determines $\snew_j$, so we rewrite it that way:
\begin{equation}
\snew_j = s_j - \Delta t\, u_j \frac{s_{j+1}-s_{j-1}}{2\Delta x} + \Delta t (a_j + w_j). \label{fd:ftcs}
\end{equation}
Note that this scheme uses 3 values at the current time to determine one value at the new time, and a \emph{stencil} diagram captures this meaning (Figure \ref{fig:stencils}, left).  The standard name for scheme \eqref{fd:ftcs} is \emph{forward-time, centered-space}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\input{tikz/ftcs.tex} \quad \input{tikz/upwind.tex}
\caption{To compute $\snew_j=s_j^{n+1}$ (circle), scheme \eqref{fd:ftcs} (left) uses 3 gridded values at the current time $t_n$ (solid), while the upwind scheme \eqref{fd:upwind} (right; $u_j>0$ case) uses just 2, depending on the flow direction.}
\label{fig:stencils}
\end{figure}

However, if we run a simulation based on scheme \eqref{fd:ftcs} then we will see wiggles which do not go away.  The possibly-surprising remedy is to revert to a less-accurate and asymmetric FD choice for the spatial derivative \cite{MortonMayers2005}.  For $\partial s/\partial x$ we take
    $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(t,x) = \frac{s(t,x) - s(t,x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x} + O(\Delta x)$$
at grid points $(t_n,x_j)$ where $u_j>0$, and otherwise ($u_j \le 0$)
    $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(t,x) = \frac{s(t,x+\Delta x) - s(t,x)}{\Delta x} + O(\Delta x).$$
This is called \emph{upwind} differencing.  These formulas have only first-order accuracy, versus the centered-difference before, but they permit (conditional) stability and the avoidance of wiggles.  Written like \eqref{fd:ftcs}, the upwind scheme is
\begin{equation}
\snew_j = s_j - \Delta t\, \frac{u_j}{\Delta x} \left\{\begin{matrix} s_j-s_{j-1}, & u_j > 0 \\ s_{j+1}-s_j, & u_j \le 0 \end{matrix}\right\} + \Delta t (a_j + w_j).  \label{fd:upwind}
\end{equation}
The stencil in Figure \ref{fig:stencils} right shows the $u_j>0$ case.  In this case the velocity feeds the ice from the left, which explains the ``upwind'' naming.

\subsection*{Time-stepping implementation}  We can now look at the code.  Upwind scheme \eqref{fd:upwind} for SKE \eqref{skeplanar} is a short Python function in the program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface.py}{\texttt{py/surface.py}}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
def explicitupwindstep(x, s, dt):
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    snew = s.copy()
    snew[1:] -= dt * u(x[1:]) * (s[1:] - s[:-1]) / dx
    snew[1:] += dt * (a(x[1:]) + w(x[1:]))
    return snew
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent In detail, \texttt{x}, \texttt{s}, and \texttt{snew} are one-dimensional NumPy \cite{Harrisetal2020} arrays, and \texttt{x} is assumed to hold an equally-spaced grid.  Separate ``data'' functions must define $a(x)$, $u(x)$, and $w(x)$.  In this implementation we need the formula for $u(x)$ to be positive, but the two cases in \eqref{fd:upwind} would be easily handled by a conditional statement.  The fact that \texttt{snew[0] = s[0]} never changes is a \emph{boundary condition} compatible with the fact that the velocity is inward at the left end (\texttt{u(x[0])}$>0$).  Also note that the left end of our glacier has zero thickness, that is, \texttt{s[0] = b(0)}.

The rest of the code \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface.py}{\texttt{py/surface.py}} is worth a look, but there is no need to print it here.  It contains formulas for the data functions, a time-stepping loop, calls to \texttt{matplotlib} to generate figures, and generous comments.  Various default choices are made for convenience and illustration, for example $\Delta t=1$ a and $\Delta x=1$ km.

\subsection*{Stability and CFL}  These first results look good (and boring).  However, easily-made modifications reveal stability issues, exposing high-spatial-frequency, rapidly-growing oscillations.  In particular, each of these separate modifications, which are a recommended exercise, generate such exploding wiggles:
\begin{itemize}
\item increase velocity: \quad $u(x)$ \, $\to$ \, $2.5\, u(x)$
\item increase resolution: \quad $\Delta x=1000$ m \, $\to$ \, $\Delta x=400$ m
\item lengthen time-steps: \quad $\Delta t = 1$ a \, $\to$ \, $\Delta t = 2.5$ a    
\end{itemize}

This kind of instability is well understood, and in fact the condition to avoid it has been known for 100 years.  The idea is that in the SKE \eqref{skeplanar}, and similar \emph{advection} equations, information about the surface travels at the speeds $\{u_j\}$.  Therefore we need to make sure that information does not travel more than one spatial distance $\Delta x$ during the time step $\Delta t$.  Equivalently, the maximum dimensionless Courant, Friedrichs, \& Lewy (CFL; \cite{CFL1928}) ratio should be at most one:
\begin{equation}
\frac{(\max_j |u_j|) \Delta t}{\Delta x} \le 1.  \label{cfl}
\end{equation}
The above destabilizing modifications each pushed this ratio over the limit.

In practice, inequality \eqref{cfl} is used to set the time step $\Delta t$ to assure stability.  At run time a simulation program sets $\Delta t = C \frac{\Delta x}{\max_j |u_j|}$, using $C=0.9$ for example, so that \eqref{cfl} holds, even when the surface velocities $u_j$ are changing in time.

\subsection*{Admissibility}  A different type of modification also breaks our method.  In the default set-up, the function $a(x)$ in \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface.py}{\texttt{py/surface.py}} is linear and decreasing  in $x$, with a zero, i.e.~an equilibrium line \cite{Cogleyetal2011}, at $1/5$ of the total length $L$.  That is, accumulation occurs over $0<x<L/5$ and ablation over $L/5<x<L$.  The modification
\begin{itemize}
\item stronger SMB: \quad $a(x)$ \, $\to$ \, $3 a(x)$  
\end{itemize}
therefore strengthens the ablation over most of the domain.  The final-time result is shown in Figure \ref{fig:inadmissdemo}, with a glacier surface which goes below the bed.  This is not good.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figs/inadmissframe050.png}
\caption{An inadmissible state, with $s<b$ in places, from stronger ablation.  See inequality \eqref{admissible}, and compare Figure \ref{fig:demo}.}
\label{fig:inadmissdemo}
\end{figure}

The bed elevation $b$ does not even appear in equations \eqref{skeplanar} and \eqref{ske}, so it would seem to be irrelevant when using the SKE to evolve the glacier geometry.  However, glaciers can end \emph{where they have ablated away}, and we need to model this real effect.

We say that a surface elevation at some time $t$ is \emph{admissible} if
\begin{equation}
s(t,x,y) \ge b(x,y) \label{admissible}
\end{equation}
for every point $x,y$ in the computational domain.  Equivalently, admissibility says that $H(t,x,y) \ge 0$; a glacier's thickness $H=s-b$ cannot be negative.

The above strengthened-SMB simulation started from an admissible state (Figure \ref{fig:demo}, top), but it evolved to an inadmissible one (Figure \ref{fig:inadmissdemo}).  Avoiding this possibility, at least in an explicit solution method, is straightforward.  Namely, we should \emph{truncate} the solution, $\snew_j = \max\{b_j, \snew_j\}$ (planar case), to force admissibility.

Inequality \eqref{admissible} complements definition \eqref{domain} of the ice domain, and it implies the \emph{free-boundary} nature of the glacier geometry problem \cite{Bueler2016,Bueler2021conservation}.  Bed elevation data $b$ can be needed even when modeling glacier surfaces using the SKE, and it is necessary when solving stress balances (Sections \ref{sec:continuum} and \ref{sec:sia}).  Note that in unglaciated areas it makes sense to say (allow) $s(t,x)=b(x)$, and this is compatible with how digital elevation models work in practice; the surface elevation is the top of the glacier if one is present, otherwise it is the top of the land.

\subsection*{Steady state}  We are regarding the SKE as a minimal model which can predict glacier geometry given data for SMB and surface velocity, and this approach works in \emph{steady state} as well.  Equation \eqref{skeplanar} with $\partial s/\partial t=0$ becomes
\begin{equation}
u \frac{\partial s}{\partial x} = a + w, \label{skeplanarsteady}
\end{equation}
and \eqref{ske} becomes $-\bu \cdot \bn_s = a$.  (Recall notation: $\bu = \left<u,v,w\right>$, $\bn_s = \big<\!-\!\frac{\partial s}{\partial x},-\,\frac{\partial s}{\partial y},1\big>$.)  Then, using an upwind FD idea like \eqref{fd:upwind}, solving \eqref{skeplanarsteady} is easier than the time-dependent case.  Specifically, because $u(x)>0$ everywhere, our FD scheme is
\begin{equation}
u_j \frac{s_{j} - s_{j-1}}{\Delta x} = a_j + w_j \qquad (\text{assumes } u_j>0), \label{fd:upwindsteady}
\end{equation}
or $s_{j} = s_{j-1} + (\Delta x/u_j) (a_j + w_j)$ as an update formula in increasing spatial indices $j$.

In code, we start from the left and have a single \texttt{for} loop:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
s = x.copy()
s[0] = b(0.0)
for j in range(1, J+1):
    s[j] = s[j-1] + (dx / u(x[j])) * (a(x[j]) + w(x[j]))
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent The same program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface.py}{\texttt{py/surface.py}} has done the job already.  Viewing the output image (\texttt{output/steady.png}) shows a result very similar to Figure \ref{fig:demo}, bottom.

We have not assured admissibility in the above loop, but this is straightforward using truncation.  The update formula inside the loop becomes
\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
    s[j] = max(b(x[j]), s[j-1] + (dx / u(x[j])) * (a(x[j]) + w(x[j])))
\end{lstlisting}

Solving the same steady-state problem in the two horizontal dimensions of a realistic glacier requires completely rethinking the solution strategy.  For that we have written a very different, and more advanced, program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/surface2d.py}{\texttt{py/surface2d.py}} which calls the Firedrake finite element library.  Now the admissibility \eqref{admissible} inequality is integrated into the weak form of the partial differential equation (PDE) problem itself, as a \emph{variational inequality} \cite[Chapter 12, and references therein]{Bueler2021}.  The reader is encouraged to explore this example, but we will not pursue this further in these notes.

\subsection*{Mass continuity equation}  Equation \eqref{ske}, the SKE in its cleanest form, relates more-or-less observable quantities on a glacier's surface.  For example, it is a preferred equation for relating measured surface velocity to SMB.  However, a different equation is often used to describe the evolution of glacier geometry, namely the \emph{mass continuity} equation, derived next.  It is essentially equivalent to \eqref{ske} because of incompressibility \eqref{incompressible}, which is mass conservation as it applies within the ice.

The derivation is clearest if we state the \emph{basal kinematical equation} first.  Consider the mass balance $m(t,x,y)$ at the basal surface $z=b(t,x,y)$, the difference between melt and freeze-on.  (Here all quantities can be time-dependent.)  The field $m$, in ice-equivalent meters per second, is positive if there is net melt.  It can be of either sign for grounded glaciers, and underneath floating ice shelves, often through thermodynamical effects.  Similar to \eqref{ske}, this kinematical equation is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial b}{\partial t} - \bu \cdot \bn_b = m,  \label{bke}
\end{equation}
where $\bn_b = \big<-\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}, -\frac{\partial b}{\partial y}, 1\big>$ is normal to the base and pointing upward, and ``$\bu$'' here denotes $\bu(t,x,y,b(t,x,y))$ (on the base surface).

If the bed elevation $b$ is time-independent ($b_t=0$) then \eqref{bke} relates the ice velocity at the base to the mass balance $m$.  For example, in the case of a grounded glacier with a flat ($\grad b=\big<\frac{\partial b}{\partial x},\frac{\partial b}{\partial y}\big>=0$) and stationary bedrock, and a melting base ($m>0$), the vertical ice velocity at the bed surface is downward: $w=-m$.

Now consider the vector horizontal velocity $\bU = \left<u,v\right>$; contrast notation for the 3D velocity $\bu=\left<u,v,w\right>$.  The horizontal \emph{ice flux}, also called \emph{discharge}, is defined to be the integral
\begin{equation}
\bq(t,x,y) = \int_{b(t,x,y)}^{s(t,x,y)} \bU(t,x,y,z)\,dz,  \label{flux}
\end{equation}
or simply $\bq=\int_b^s\bU\,dz$ if we suppress variables.  The flux is a vector with two components, $\bq=\left<q_1,q_2\right>$.  It is often written $\bq=\bar\bU H$ where $H=s-b$ is the ice thickness; it follows that $\bar\bU=H^{-1} \int_b^s\bU\,dz$ is the vertically-averaged horizontal velocity.

Finally we can state the mass continuity equation itself,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} + \Div \bq = a - m.  \label{masscont}
\end{equation}
Note that if the bed is stationary ($\partial b/\partial t=0$) then we can equivalently write ``$\partial s/\partial t$'' on the left, but the most common way to think about this equation is as describing how a layer of ice changes thickness, and thus volume, instead of in terms of elevation.  The divergence here is in horizontal variables, so $\Div\bq= \frac{\partial q_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial q_2}{\partial y}$.

The net function $a-m$ on the right is the \emph{climatic-basal mass balance} \cite{Cogleyetal2011}.  This combination of the surface and basal mass balances $a$ and $m$ determines how much mass is lost or gained in a column within the glacier.

Since equation \eqref{masscont} looks different from the others, we give a brief derivation.  We need the \emph{Leibniz rule} for differentiating integrals,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_{g(x)}^{f(x)} k(x,y)\,dy\right) = k(x,f(x))\, f'(x) - k(x,g(x)) \, g'(x) + \int_{g(x)}^{f(x)} \frac{\partial k}{\partial x}(x,y)\,dy,  \label{leibniz}
\end{equation}
where $x$ should be regarded as a generic independent variable, and $k,f,g$ as generic differentiable functions.  Note that ``$y$'' is an index (variable) of integration, thus the results do not depend on it.

We apply the Leibniz rule to compute the gradient (partial derivatives) of the flux \eqref{flux}, paying attention to which functions depend on which independent variables:
\begin{align*}
(\Div \bq) (t,x,y) &= \bU(t,x,y,s(t,x,y)) \cdot \grad s(t,x,y) - \bU(t,x,y,b(t,x,y)) \cdot \grad b(t,x,y) \\
   &\qquad + \int_{b(t,x,y)}^{s(t,x,y)} (\Div \bU)(t,x,y,z)\,dz.
\end{align*}
Divergences $\Div$ here are in the horizontal variables $x,y$.  By incompressibility \eqref{incompressible} we can write the integrand as a vertical derivative of the vertical velocity:
    $$\Div \bU = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = - \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}.$$
Thus, continuing the calculation, indicating surface values by ``$\bU|_s$'' etc., suppressing variables, and applying the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have
    $$\Div \bq = \bU|_s \cdot \grad s - \bU|_b \cdot \grad b - \int_b^s \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\,dz = \bU|_s \cdot \grad s - \bU|_b \cdot \grad b - w|_s + w|_b.$$
The expressions on the right are familiar from the kinematical equations \eqref{ske} and \eqref{bke}, and in fact if we use the 3D velocity $\bu=\left<u,v,w\right>$, and our normal vector notations, and those kinematical equations, then we have
    $$\Div \bq = -\bu|_s \cdot \bn_s + \bu|_b \cdot \bn_b = a - \frac{\partial s}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial b}{\partial t} - m.$$
This says $\Div \bq = (a-m) - \partial H/\partial t$, which is mass continuity \eqref{masscont}.

At this point we have three equations which might be used to describe the evolving geometry of a glacier:
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\emph{\roman{enumi})}}
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item the surface kinematical equation (SKE) \eqref{ske},
  \item the basal kinematical equation \eqref{bke}, and
  \item the mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont}.  
  \end{enumerate}
We derived the third using the first two and incompressibility.  Actually, any \emph{pair} of these three equations implies the third, using incompressibility and the Leibniz rule to make the connections.

In designing a numerical glacier simulation, which equations to use for time-dependent geometry is largely a matter of taste and tradition.  The most common choice in the literature is to use mass continuity \eqref{masscont} as the primary equation, and to interpret the basal kinematical relation \eqref{bke} in a somewhat ad hoc manner.  Our main point here is that we can treat these equations as tools, and be free to use them.

\subsection*{Understanding planar mass continuity}  It helps to illustrate mass continuity in the same $x,z$ flow-line plane we used earlier.  Suppose for simplicity that the base is flat ($b=0$), so the thickness and surface elevation are the same thing ($H=s$), and suppose there is no basal mass balance ($m=0$).  In this planar case the horizontal velocity is a scalar function $U$, and its vertical average $\bar U = H^{-1} \int_0^{H} U\,dz$ and the flux $q = \int_0^{H} U\,dz = \bar U\, H$ are also scalar.

The ice flux $q = \bar U\, H$ is the rate of mass flow through the sides of a part of the glacier, for example at locations $x_1$ and $x_2$ as shown in Figure \ref{fig:slabmasscontfig}.  Therefore mass can be added by SMB $a$, distributed along the upper surface, or by a \emph{difference} in the flux between the left and right sides.  That is, the flow-line area $A$ of this part of the glacier changes according to the sum of certain boundary contributions:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dA}{dt} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} a\,dx + \bar U_1\, H_1 - \bar U_2\, H_2. \label{masscontintegrated}
\end{equation}
In three-dimensions, \eqref{masscontintegrated} would become an equation for $dV/dt$, the rate of change of the volume of a part of the glacier, which would have more sides.

\onefigsize{slabmasscontfig}{Mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont} can be interpreted as giving the rate of change of the time-dependent area $A$.}{2.0in}

Equation \eqref{masscontintegrated} is an integrated form of mass continuity.  To determine the derivative (and planar) form, suppose the width $\Delta x=x_2-x_1$ is small, thus $A\approx \Delta x\, H$.  If we divide \eqref{masscontintegrated} by $\Delta x$ and take $\Delta x \to 0$ then we get
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = a - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\bar U\, H\right) \label{masscontplanar}
\end{equation}
because $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (\int_{x_1}^{x_2} a\,dx)/\Delta x = a$ is the average value of the SMB over the small interval $[x_1,x_2]$, and $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (\bar U_2\, H_2 - \bar U_1\, H_1)/\Delta x = \partial(\bar U\,H)/\partial x$ is a (partial) derivative.  We have re-derived, and perhaps understood better, equation \eqref{masscont} in the planar case.


\section{Shallow ice approximation}  \label{sec:sia}

\subsection*{Shallowness of glaciers and ice sheets}  Glacier ice has several outstanding properties as a fluid.  As explained in Section \ref{sec:continuum}, it is slow and non-Newtonian.  Also, glaciers are subject to contact slip, i.e.~basal sliding, along a hard-to-observe boundary.  However, it is the typical \emph{shallowness} of glacier geometry, at least for large glaciers and ice sheets, which allows us to use a simpler stress balance model, an approximation to the full Glen-Stokes system, which enables faster simulations.  Because ice sheet geometry is both shallow and slow, the problem has a distinctive \emph{diffusive} character.

Figure \ref{fig:green-transect} shows a cross-section of the Greenland ice sheet.  This vertically-exaggerated view is more familiar from the glaciological literature, but without such exaggeration the ice sheet is revealed to be a thin blanket, barely three-dimensional.  More precisely, ice sheets are \emph{shallow} in the sense that the dimensionless ratio $\eps = [H]/[L]$, between typical scales for thickness $[H]$ and horizontal (length) $[L]$, is small.  For this Greenland example, $[H]=2000$ m and $[L]=1000$ km are representative, so $\eps = 0.002$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=80mm,height=35mm]{green-transect}
\caption{A cross-section of the Greenland ice sheet at $71^\circ$ N latitude, 760 km wide by 3200 m thick.  The slightly-thick horizontal line (bottom; red) shows the same cross-section as the blue and green surfaces, but without the vertical exaggeration.}
\label{fig:green-transect}
\end{figure}

The non-sliding, isothermal \emph{shallow ice approximation} (SIA; \cite{Bueler2016,GreveBlatter2009,SchoofHewitt2013}) is an effective model for shallow glaciers, as long as they are not dominated by basal sliding.  Mountain glaciers have much larger $\eps$ ratios, but they too are often modeled within an SIA framework.  A model of a small portion of an ice sheet may have a large $\eps$ ratio, so it might not use the SIA.  If there is substantial basal sliding, or the ice is floating, then a different shallow model, the \emph{shallow shelf} or \emph{membrane} approximation \cite{SchoofHewitt2013}, is more appropriate.  Hybrid shallow models \cite{BuelerBrown2009}, that combine the two approximations, are in widespread use, and comprehensive models couple these with conservation of energy \cite{Aschwandenetal2012}.  However, these notes will not plunge into the thicket of modern modeling choices.

\subsection*{Velocity formulas in the SIA}  The label ``SIA'' is put on several equations.  First it refers to certain simplified velocity formulas.  The principled way to derive these formulas starts from the full Glen-Stokes stress balance \eqref{incompressibleagain}--\eqref{flowlaw}, and then applies a small parameter argument \cite[for example]{Fowler1997}, rescaling the variables using $\eps = [H]/[L]$, and then dropping terms which are small when $\eps$ is small.  However, the same destination can be reached by a lazier approach, adopted here.  The SIA can be regarded as using the slab-on-a-slope result \eqref{uslab}, as valid even for general glaciers, not just uniform slabs.  Actually, we need two assumptions for this to work: \emph{i)} the surface $s$ and bed $b$ elevation functions must be smooth, and \emph{ii)} a small-angle approximation must be adequate to replace $\sin\alpha$ in \eqref{uslab} with the surface slope $|\grad s|$.  Regarding \emph{ii)}, $|\sin \alpha| \approx |\tan\alpha| = |\grad s|$ when $\alpha$ is small (Figure \ref{fig:slab}).  A key idea is that the shear stresses are then linear in depth and proportional to $|\grad s|$.

For a general Glen exponent $n$, equation \eqref{uslab} becomes the following SIA formula for the horizontal velocity $\bU(t,x,y,z)$, which is $\bU=\left<u,v\right>$ in components:
\begin{equation}
\bU = \bU_0 - \frac{2}{n+1} A (\rho g)^n \left((s-b)^{n+1} - (s-z)^{n+1}\right) |\grad s|^{n-1} \grad s. \label{sia:ugeneral}
\end{equation}
The first minus sign in \eqref{sia:ugeneral} is remembered by noting that ice flows downhill, that is, opposite the $\grad s$ direction.  When using SKE \eqref{ske} to evolve geometry under the non-sliding SIA theory---see the next Section---only the surface value is needed:
\begin{equation}
\bU|_s = - \frac{2}{n+1} A (\rho g)^n (s-b)^{n+1} |\grad s|^{n-1} \grad s. \label{sia:usurface}
\end{equation}
Derive \eqref{sia:usurface} by substituting $\bU_0=0$ and $z=s$ into \eqref{sia:ugeneral}.

Vertical integration yields two additional formulas.  First, vertical integration of \eqref{sia:ugeneral}, using the power rule $\int z^{n+1}\,dz = z^{n+2}/(n+2) + c$, yields a formula for the ice flux:
\begin{equation}
\bq = - \frac{2}{n+2} A (\rho g)^n (s-b)^{n+2} |\grad s|^{n-1} \grad s. \label{sia:q:early}
\end{equation}
(Compare equation \eqref{flux}.)  Second, we get the surface value of the vertical velocity:
\begin{align}
w|_s &= \bU|_s \cdot \grad s - \Div \bq   \label{sia:wsurface:early} \\
  &= - \frac{2}{n+1} A (\rho g)^n (s-b)^{n+1} |\grad s|^{n+1} + \Div \left(\frac{2}{n+2} A (\rho g)^n (s-b)^{n+2} |\grad s|^{n-1} \grad s\right).  \notag
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{sia:wsurface:early} is derived like the mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont}.  One uses incompressibility \eqref{incompressibleagain}, $w|_b=0$, and vertical integration to express $w$ at the surface, and then the Leibniz rule \eqref{leibniz} and $\bU_0=0$.  The second line follows from \eqref{sia:usurface} and \eqref{sia:q:early}.

The formulas for $\bq$ and $w|_s$ have a common scalar \emph{diffusivity} coefficient function:
\begin{equation}
\Dsia = \frac{2}{n+2} A (\rho g)^n (s-b)^{n+2} |\grad s|^{n-1}. \label{sia:D}
\end{equation}
This positive coefficient appears in \eqref{sia:q:early} and \eqref{sia:wsurface:early}, and now we can write them cleanly:
\begin{align}
\bq  &= - \Dsia \grad s. \label{sia:q} \\
w|_s &= \bU|_s \cdot \grad s + \Div \left(\Dsia \grad s\right).  \label{sia:wsurface}
\end{align}
In the remainder of this section we will try to understand these SIA formulas, and see how they might be applied in examples, and in simulations of evolving glaciers.

\subsection*{Example: Computing SIA velocities for a given geometry}    If the glacier geometry is known then the above formulas compute the surface velocity and the flux.  We will do this approximately using finite differences (FD; Section \ref{sec:masscont}), as needed for the time-dependent SIA simulations to follow, but it can be done analytically by differentiating the surface elevation function.

For planar geometry given by known functions $b(x)$ and $s(x)$, either steady or a snapshot in time, \eqref{sia:usurface} simplifies to this formula for horizontal surface velocity:
\begin{equation}
u|_s = - \frac{2}{n+1} A (\rho g)^n (s-b)^{n+1} \left|\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}\right|^{n-1} \frac{\partial s}{\partial x}. \label{sia:usurface:planar}
\end{equation}
For example, suppose $b$ is a simple incline and $s$ is a gently-oscillating surface, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:uw} (top).  Where this surface is (nearly) flat we expect $u|_s\approx 0$, while on the steeper parts we expect significant rightward flow $u|_s>0$.

\onefigsize{uw}{Top panel: glacier geometry.  Bottom panel: FD approximations of the horizontal ($\circ$) and vertical ($+$) surface velocity in the SIA.}{4.0in}

Our FD approximation of \eqref{sia:usurface:planar} will compute the surface slope at the \emph{staggered grid} points $x_{j+1/2}$, which are half-way between the regular grid locations $\{x_j\}$; see Section \ref{sec:masscont} for grid notation.  Using the staggered point achieves second-order accuracy because the function evaluations are centered around it:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(x_{j+1/2}) = \frac{s(x_{j+1})-s(x_j)}{\Delta x} + O((\Delta x)^2).  \label{fd:slope}
\end{equation}
Averaging the ice thicknesses, symmetrically to the staggered point, is also needed, to maintain the second-order accuracy.  The velocity approximation is
\begin{equation}
u_{j+1/2} = - \frac{2}{n+1} A (\rho g)^n \left(\frac{H_j + H_{j+1}}{2}\right)^{n+1} \left|\frac{s_{j+1}-s_j}{\Delta x}\right|^{n-1} \frac{s_{j+1}-s_j}{\Delta x} \label{fd:usurface:planar}
\end{equation}
where $H_j = s_j - b_j$.  Here is the Python function for \eqref{fd:usurface:planar}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
def u(x, b, s):
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    dsdx = (s[1:] - s[:-1]) / dx
    Hstag = ((s[:-1] - b[:-1]) + (s[1:] - b[1:])) / 2.0
    C_u = (2.0 / (n + 1)) * A * (rhoi * g) ** n
    return -C_u * Hstag ** (n + 1) * abs(dsdx) ** (n - 1) * dsdx
\end{lstlisting}

The diffusivity $\Dsia$ is computed from \eqref{sia:D} at staggered points as follows:
\begin{equation}
\Dsia_{j+1/2} = \frac{2}{n+2} A (\rho g)^n \left(\frac{H_j + H_{j+1}}{2}\right)^{n+2} \left|\frac{s_{j+1}-s_j}{\Delta x}\right|^{n-1}. \label{fd:Dsia:planar}
\end{equation}
The Python is short, and similar to the horizontal velocity:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
def diffusiveD(x, b, s):
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    dsdx = (s[1:] - s[:-1]) / dx
    Hstag = ((s[:-1] - b[:-1]) + (s[1:] - b[1:])) / 2.0
    C_D = (2.0 / (n + 2)) * A * (rhoi * g) ** n
    return C_D * Hstag ** (n + 2) * abs(dsdx) ** (n - 1)
\end{lstlisting}
Computing $\bq$ at staggered grid points requires only a small modification, but we will not specifically need that here.

To compute the surface vertical velocity $w|_s$ we evaluate \eqref{sia:wsurface} using another centered difference, which yields values on the (interior) regular grid points:
\begin{equation}
w_j = \frac{\phi_{j-1/2} + \phi_{j+1/2}}{2} + \frac{\Dsia_{j+1/2} (s_{j+1}-s_j) - \Dsia_{j-1/2} (s_{j}-s_{j-1})}{\Delta x^2}, \label{fd:wsurface:planar}
\end{equation}
where
	$$\phi_{j+1/2} = u_{j+1/2} \frac{s_{j+1}-s_j}{\Delta x} \approx \left(u|_s \frac{\partial s}{\partial x}\right)_{j+1/2}.$$
The short Python function which computes \eqref{fd:wsurface:planar} is not shown.

Run \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/shallow.py}{\texttt{py/shallow.py}} as follows.  It calls the above functions, for a particular geometry, and it generates Figure \ref{fig:uw}:

\end{Verbatim}

\medskip
What does Figure \ref{fig:uw} show?  Because of gravity the flow is faster where the surface is steeper, and the horizontal velocity reflects this, as expected.  The surface vertical velocity is more interesting.  Through incompressibility, it causes the surface to rise in dips and fall at humps.  This is a key feature of ice flow: it is \emph{smoothing}.  The vertical velocity tends to remove details of surface topography, at least at the large scales covered by the SIA.  Gravity and incompressibility combine to generate ice surface motion which generates smoother surfaces under SKE evolution, at least when the SMB data is also smooth (in space and time).

Figure \ref{fig:green-transect} shows the result for a real ice sheet; the surface is much smoother than the base.  Ice-penetrating radar in the 21st century has revealed widespread rough bedrock topography under ice sheets, making it even more obvious that their surfaces are smoother than their beds.  Numerically solving the time-dependent SIA, our next task, will also make the smoothing effect clear.


\section{Time-dependent glacier simulations from the SIA}  \label{sec:evolution}

\subsection*{Diffusion equations for geometry}  Section \ref{sec:masscont} presented two equations which model the evolution of a glacier's shape: the surface kinematical equation (SKE) \eqref{ske} for the surface elevation $s$, and the mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont} for the thickness $H$.  If we decide to compute velocities and fluxes from the non-sliding shallow ice approximation (SIA; Section \ref{sec:sia}), which approximates the Glen-Stokes system of Section \ref{sec:continuum}, then these evolution equations become time-dependent glacier models.  Unlike the minimal SKE-based ``model'' in Section \ref{sec:masscont}, these SIA geometry-evolution models are self-contained.

In the current Section we will demonstrate this strategy, combining equations from Sections \ref{sec:masscont} and \ref{sec:sia} to build the simplest well-posed\footnote{In a technical sense the well-posedness problem is only partly solved \cite{JouvetBueler2012}.} evolution model in glaciology.  Simulations built this way start from a known glacier geometry and apply a (generally) time-dependent climate, an SMB function.  They generate the geometry at future times, which can be regarded as predictions.

All SIA-based geometry-evolution equations simplify to essentially the same \emph{diffusion equation}.  On the one hand, recall the SKE \eqref{ske}, namely
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} - \bu|_s \cdot \bn_s = a.  \label{skeagain}
\end{equation}
Here $\bu|_s=\left<u|_s,v|_s,w|_s\right>$ is the 3D surface velocity and $\bn_s = \left<-\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}, -\frac{\partial s}{\partial y}, \,1\right>$ is a normal vector.  From equation \eqref{sia:wsurface} the surface motion term has a divergence form:
	$$\bu|_s \cdot \bn_s = -\bU|_s \cdot \grad s + w|_s = \Div\left(\Dsia \grad s\right).$$
(Note that $\bU|_s \cdot \grad s$ is a 2D dot product.)  Recall that $\Dsia$ is given by equation \eqref{sia:D}, and Section \ref{sec:sia} provides a Python function which computes it.

On the other hand, recall mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont} for the thickness $H=s-b$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial H}{\partial t} + \Div \bq = a - m.  \label{masscontagain}
\end{equation}
The SIA ice flux $\bq$ is from equation \eqref{sia:q}: $\bq = - \Dsia \grad s$.

These (non-sliding) SIA substitutions create very-similar diffusion equations, the boxed equations in the following diagram.

\medskip
Such similar forms are not a surprise, actually.  Section \ref{sec:masscont} showed that our starting equations \eqref{skeagain} and \eqref{masscontagain} are equivalent if we assume incompressibility \eqref{incompressibleagain} and a basal kinematical equation \eqref{bke}.    Both equations \eqref{diffusion:ske} and \eqref{diffusion:masscont} involve the surface elevation (via the slope $\grad s$) and the ice thickness (through $\Dsia$, equation \eqref{sia:D}).  The apparent $s$-vs-$H$ distinction is not that important, but the surface elevation $s$ tends to be a smoother geometry function in time and space than the thickness $H$ (e.g.~Figure \ref{fig:green-transect}).

An apparent difference between the boxed equations is that \eqref{diffusion:ske} does not refer to the \emph{basal} mass balance $m$ (Section \ref{sec:masscont}).  The original SKE \eqref{ske} isolates the surface evolution, while the mass continuity equation \eqref{masscont} always involves the whole ice column.  On the other hand, the derivation of \eqref{diffusion:ske} used \eqref{sia:D}, via an assumption of zero basal velocity, and if that derivation were generalized appropriately then the basal mass balance $m$ would reappear.

The two models \eqref{diffusion:ske} and \eqref{diffusion:masscont} are similar in another respect: they must be solved subject to a geometrical inequality constraint.  That is, the surface must remain above the bed ($s\ge b$) and the thickness must not be negative ($H\ge 0$).

Historically-speaking, the diffusion form \eqref{diffusion:masscont} of the mass continuity equation allowed numerical ice sheet modeling to get started.  Diffusion equations, especially the \emph{heat equation}, are clich\'ed topics in numerical analysis textbooks, for example in \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005}.  By inventing an FD scheme for the diffusivity in \eqref{diffusion:masscont}, Mary-Anne Mahaffy \cite{Mahaffy1976} started the field with a 1976 numerical model of the Barnes ice cap (Baffin Island, Canada).

\subsection*{Nonlinear and degenerate diffusivity}  Equation \eqref{sia:D} gives the diffusivity $\Dsia$, which appears in the diffusion equations \eqref{diffusion:ske} and \eqref{diffusion:masscont}:
\begin{equation}
\Dsia = \frac{2}{n+2} A (\rho g)^n (s-b)^{n+2} |\grad s|^{n-1}. \label{sia:Dagain}
\end{equation}
Independent of the power $n$, the units of $\Dsia$ reduce to square meters per second, as otherwise equations \eqref{diffusion:ske} and \eqref{diffusion:masscont} would not be dimensionally correct.  It follows that the units of the ice softness $A$ will depend on $n$ (Table \ref{tab:notation}).

Equation \eqref{sia:Dagain} expresses the dominant macroscopic effect of the Glen flow law \eqref{flowlaw}, and it is at the heart of why glaciology is a (highly!) \emph{nonlinear} science.  The diffusivity is not a constant, nor a known-in-advance function, as it is in linear textbook examples \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005}.  Instead, for $n=3$ it scales with the $5$th power of thickness and the $2$nd power of surface slope.  Even when $n=1$, which is the Newtonian fluid case where diffusivity is independent of surface slope, it still depends on the $3$rd power of thickness.

The local value of diffusivity $\Dsia$ is the rate of smoothing of the surface elevation, over adjacent areas, caused by ice flow.  Formula \eqref{sia:Dagain} returns a large value when the ice is both thick and steep.  Ice will flow downhill, away from surface maxima, and it will converge toward surface minima, most strongly at such locations.

However, it is worth noting some conceptual and practical concerns about \eqref{diffusion:ske} or \eqref{diffusion:masscont} as diffusion PDEs, given the nonlinearities in formula \eqref{sia:Dagain}:
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\emph{\roman{enumi})}}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The derivation of the SIA model is inaccurate when significant longitudinal (membrane) stresses are present.  This is distinctly true when ice is floating or sliding, but also over shorter ranges when the flow is confined by terrain.  In such cases the SIA approximation of the true solution of the Glen-Stokes model equations \eqref{incompressibleagain}--\eqref{flowlaw} may be completely inadequate.
\item The diffusivity $\Dsia$ depends on the solution, and thus PDEs \eqref{diffusion:ske} and \eqref{diffusion:masscont} are nonlinear.  Numerical methods are therefore more difficult to implement, and harder to analyze and debug.
\item The diffusivity $\Dsia$ goes to zero at margins, where $H=s-b\to 0$, and at divides and domes, where $|\grad s|\to 0$.  That is, the diffusion process \emph{degenerates}.  The solutions $s$ and $H$ of \eqref{diffusion:ske} or \eqref{diffusion:masscont} are generally not smooth at these locations, though they will be continuous everywhere if $b$ is.  Smoothness of $s$ and $H$ is lost at margins and domes even when the bed $b$ and SMB $a$ data are smooth.  The largest numerical geometry errors arise at such locations.
\end{enumerate}
Item \emph{i)} concerns a deficiency of the SIA model, not just our diffusion-based understanding of it.  All of these concerns are research topics in mathematical glaciology.

\subsection*{Explicit scheme for the geometry SIA}  Having thought about the form and meaning of these equations, let us apply finite differences (FD) and write code.

Consider the planar case of equation \eqref{diffusion:ske}.  This is the simplest combination of the SKE and the SIA stress balance:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left(\Dsia \frac{\partial s}{\partial x}\right) = a.  \label{diffusion:ske:planar}
\end{equation}

The simplest scheme for \eqref{diffusion:ske:planar} applies forward differences in time (Euler method) and centered differences in space.  We will treat the diffusivity as a coefficient which varies in time and space, evaluated on the staggered grid, $D_{j+1/2} = \Dsia(t_n,x_{j+1/2})$.  Recall from Section \ref{sec:masscont} the notation $s_j \approx s(t_n,x_j)$ and $\snew_j\approx s(t_{n+1},x_j)$, and now we write $a_j= a(t_n,x_j)$.  Here is the scheme:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\snew_j - s_j}{\Delta t} - \frac{D_{j+1/2} (s_{j+1} - s_j)/\Delta x - D_{j-1/2} (s_j - s_{j-1})/\Delta x}{\Delta x} = a_j.  \label{fd:diffusion:planar:early}
\end{equation}

The staggered-grid diffusivities $D_{j+1/2}$ are exactly the values of $\Dsia$ computed in Section \ref{sec:sia} by \texttt{diffusiveD()}, so we will re-use that Python function.  The slopes $(s_{j+1} - s_j)/\Delta x$ use approximation \eqref{fd:slope}.  Note that our centered-difference approximation of the outer derivative in \eqref{diffusion:ske:planar} can be regarded as differencing staggered-grid values of the flux $\bq=-\Dsia \grad s$, an idea which generalizes to two horizontal dimensions \cite{Bueler2016}.

Unlike the advection problem in Section \ref{sec:masscont}, a forward-time centered-space scheme \eqref{fd:diffusion:planar:early} is acceptable for a diffusion equation like \eqref{diffusion:ske:planar}.  It is \emph{conditionally stable} \cite{MortonMayers2005}, about which more below.  For implementation we write \eqref{fd:diffusion:planar:early} as an update,
\begin{equation}
\snew_j = s_j + \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x^2} \left[D_{j+1/2}\, (s_{j+1} - s_j) - D_{j-1/2}\, (s_j - s_{j-1})\right] + \Delta t\,a_j,  \label{fd:diffusion:planar}
\end{equation}
which becomes this Python in the $a=0$ case we will be testing:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
def geometrystep(x, s, D, dt):
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    dsdx = (s[1:] - s[:-1]) / dx
    snew = s.copy()
    snew[1:-1] += dt * (D[1:] * dsdx[1:] - D[:-1] * dsdx[:-1]) / dx
    return snew
\end{lstlisting}

The above code freezes the surface elevation at both ends, that is, \texttt{snew[0]=s[0]} and \texttt{snew[J+1]=s[J+1]}.  In Section \ref{sec:masscont} we needed a single boundary condition for the SKE, which was a one-way advection (velocity-is-given paradigm).  Now our velocities are from the SIA, and we have a second-order (in space) diffusion problem, so these Dirichlet boundary values are appropriate \cite{MortonMayers2005}.

The time axis is $t\in [0,t_f]$, with final time $t_f$.  The example below uses $t_f=40$ a.  As explained momentarily, the following time-stepping loop is \emph{adaptive} in the sense that $\Delta t$ is re-computed at each step, based on the current state of the solution:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
t = 0
for k in range(maxsteps):
    D = diffusiveD(x, b, s)
    dt = getdt(t, tf, D.max())  # adaptive time step length
    if tf - t < 1.0e-6:  # normal stopping criterion
        break
    s = geometrystep(x, b, s, D, dt)
    t += dt
\end{lstlisting}
Normal termination is by the \texttt{break}, once $t$ is within $10^{-6}$ seconds of the final time, to allow for rounding error, while \texttt{maxsteps} merely protects against an infinite loop.

\subsection*{Adaptive time-stepping for stability}  If \eqref{diffusion:ske:planar} were a linear PDE, so that the staggered-grid diffusivities $D_{j+1/2}$ did not depend on the solution $s$, then it is well-known \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005} that a sufficient condition for scheme stability is
\begin{equation}
   \frac{(\max_j D_{j+1/2})\,\Delta t}{\Delta x^2} \le \frac{1}{2} \quad \iff \quad \Delta t \le \frac{\Delta x^2}{2 (\max_j D_{j+1/2})}.  \label{linearstabcrit}
\end{equation}
This criterion is comparable\footnote{Condition \eqref{linearstabcrit} is sometimes called ``CFL'' also, but it applies to a different problem.} to the CFL condition \eqref{cfl}, which was for the explicit upwind scheme applied to the advection problem in Section \ref{sec:masscont}.

However, using condition \eqref{linearstabcrit} in an adaptive time-stepping scheme will not give stability for a glacier simulation.  PDE \eqref{diffusion:ske:planar} is actually nonlinear, because of the dependence of $\Dsia$ on the solution, equation \eqref{sia:Dagain}, and a different analysis is needed.  That analysis \cite{Hindmarsh2001} concludes that $1/(2n)$ should replace ``$1/2$'' in \eqref{linearstabcrit}, so we compute the time-step as follows:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=PythonPlus, xleftmargin=4mm]
def getdt(t, tf, maxD):
    c_D = 1.0 / 6.0  # coefficient from Hindmarsh (2001), for n=3
    dt_D = c_D * dx**2 / maxD  # apply diffusive condition
    return min([dt_D, tf - t])  # do not step past end of [0,tf]
\end{lstlisting}


\subsection*{Example: A very rough start}  These pieces go into a Python program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/dome.py}{\texttt{py/dome.py}} which simulates a time-dependent glacier using the SIA.  The data (climate) is highly-simplified: the bed is flat ($b=0$) and the SMB is zero ($a=0$; also $m=0$).  A flat bed means that $H=s$ and diffusion equations \eqref{diffusion:ske} and \eqref{diffusion:masscont} are identical.  The focus in this simulation is on how SIA ice flow evolves glacier shape.

On the other hand we generate the initial surface elevation \emph{randomly}.  The geometry is initially horrible, compared to real glaciers.  Run this experiment as follows:

\smallskip
\begin{Verbatim}[xleftmargin=4mm]
$ python3 dome.py 
grid: J=80 intervals, spacing dx=500.0 m
starting time-stepping ...
took 1814 steps to reach final time 40.0000 a
writing results to output/dome.png and output/timesteps.png
\end{Verbatim}
%$

\medskip
The evolving geometry is shown in Figure \ref{fig:dome}.  The initial surface generates very large stresses in the SIA model, and thus strain rates, and so the geometry change rate $|\partial s/\partial t|$ is large at the beginning.  Recall that the SIA formula \eqref{sia:Dagain}, for $\Dsia$, generates large values when the ice is thick and the surface slope is steep, which certainly applies during the early part of the simulation.  However, the ice flow is aggressively smoothing and the final surface elevation is a surprisingly symmetric and smooth dome shape, to which we will return soon.  Also note that, because of conserved mass, the \emph{glacier margin} advances as the central heights fall; the free boundary moves.

\onefigsize{dome}{Evolution of surface elevation (and thickness) under the SIA model, from a very rough start.}{4.0in}

The other output from the run, Figure \ref{fig:timesteps}, graphs the time step $\Delta t$ over time.  At the start the stability criteria sees enormous computed diffusivities so the time steps are correspondingly short.  Toward the end of the run the time steps stabilize, at order of a month for this resolution.  Given the crazy start, this example also demonstrates the \emph{robustness} of the diffusion-based adaptive time-stepping approach.

\onefigsize{timesteps}{Time step length $\Delta t$, over time, from the simulation which produced Figure \ref{fig:dome}.}{3.0in}

Modifications of the program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/dome.py}{\texttt{py/dome.py}} are recommended exercises.  Runs become unstable if the time step $\Delta t$ is too large, although the coefficient $1/6$ can be increased quite a bit without obvious instabilities.  One exercise is to throw out the adaptive time-stepping scheme entirely, but then it becomes clear why we want it.  Fixing a sufficiently-short time step to permit stability will generate many more time steps and thus long run times.

The most valuable exercise is to increase the spatial resolution, e.g.~by doubling the number $J$ of subintervals.  Our program does $O(J)$ arithmetic to compute each time step, and so of course the simulation gets more computationally expensive under spatial refinement.  However, the adaptive scheme \emph{also} reduces $\Delta t$, thus the number of time steps increases.  An easy exercise shows that a stability criterion like \eqref{linearstabcrit} converts a doubling of $J$ into an increase in arithmetic (\emph{flops}), for the whole simulation to a fixed final time $t_f$, by a factor of eight!

Adaptive time-stepping is strongly recommended to maintain the stability of glacier simulations, especially because of the strong nonlinearities in ice flow.  This recommendation extends to comprehensive glacier and ice sheet models containing multiple time scales and processes \cite{BuelerBrown2009}.

\subsection*{Implicit schemes for glacier evolution}  An alternate approach to creating stable numerical schemes is \emph{implicitness}.  For example, an implicit \emph{backward Euler} scheme corresponding to \eqref{fd:diffusion:planar:early} is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\snew_j - s_j}{\Delta t} - \frac{D_{j+1/2} (\snew_{j+1} - \snew_j)/\Delta x - D_{j-1/2} (\snew_j - \snew_{j-1})/\Delta x}{\Delta x} = a_j.  \label{fd:diffusion:planar:beuler}
\end{equation}
Here the unknown surface elevations $\snew_j$ appear in multiple places in the equation.  We cannot write it as an explicit update scheme like \eqref{fd:diffusion:planar}.  Instead we must solve algebraic equations, simultaneously for all the values $\{\snew_j\}$, at each time step.  Nonetheless the stability benefits of implicitness are substantial, compensating for increased program complexity.  For linear diffusions, backward Euler scheme \eqref{fd:diffusion:planar:beuler} is \emph{unconditionally stable}, meaning that arbitrarily long time steps are allowed without instability \cite{MortonMayers2005}.

Implicitness comes with caveats.  First, long time steps, i.e.~large $\Delta t$ values, always imply an accuracy cost, because the approximation $\partial s/\partial t \approx (\snew_j - s_j)/\Delta t$ becomes poor.  Therefore accuracy limits time steps, not just stability.  Second, and more important, implicit schemes are more expensive in a per-time-step sense because the unknown solution values must be treated as a vector in a (large) linear or nonlinear system of equations, which must be formed and solved at each time step.

Note that scheme \eqref{fd:diffusion:planar:beuler} is really only \emph{semi-implicit} because it uses staggered-grid diffusivities which were evaluated, by formula \eqref{sia:Dagain}, from the current-time surface elevations $s_j$.  The advantage is that only a linear system needs to be solved at each time step for such a semi-implicit scheme.  A fully-implicit scheme would use the updated values $\snew_j$ there as well, and this would require the solution of a system on nonlinear equations at each time step, for instance by Newton's method \cite{Bueler2021}.

Despite the strong nonlinearities, fully implicit schemes are implementable when appropriate high-performance solver tools are used \cite{Bueler2016}.  Because there is a tradeoff between easy implementability, for adaptive explicit schemes, versus improved stability, implicit evolution schemes have not made it into widely-used glacier models.  Especially for Stokes-type models, the tradeoff depends on the poorly-understood character of geometry evolution, and the jury is still out on whether they are the better choice \cite{Bueler2023}.  Most present-day numerical glacier and ice sheet models use explicit time-stepping.

\subsection*{Halfar's exact, attracting solution}   The final shape in Figure \ref{fig:dome} is a smooth and symmetrical dome, but this is no accident.  It turns out that \emph{any} flat-bed ($b=0$) and zero SMB ($a=0$) glacier will asymptotically approach this dome shape.

In 1981 Peter Halfar published a time-dependent SIA solution \cite{Halfar1981}, and he showed that any rough surface will evolve toward this solution.\footnote{The proof, a linear asymptotic analysis, applies to any surface that is not too far from the solution, but numerical results show that large perturbations will also converge to the Halfar dome solution.}  A second paper \cite{Halfar1983} extended these results to two horizontal dimensions.  The Halfar solutions are both exact and \emph{attracting}, and thus worth knowing.

Consider the PDE you get by inserting \eqref{sia:Dagain} into \eqref{diffusion:ske}.  Then setting $a=b=0$, and naming the constant $\Gamma = \frac{2}{n+2} A (\rho g)^n$, we have this simplified nonlinear diffusion equation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} = \Div \left(\Gamma s^{n+2} |\grad s|^{n-1} \grad s\right).  \label{sia:pureforhalfar}
\end{equation}

Halfar found the \emph{similarity solution} of this SIA equation by applying scaling arguments, as follows for the planar case.  Because $a=0$ the ice mass is conserved, so the input ($x$) and output ($s$) lengths can be re-scaled by the same unknown power of $t$:
\begin{equation}
x = t^{\alpha} \tilde x, \qquad s(t,x)=t^{-\alpha} \phi(\tilde x) \label{halfarscalings}
\end{equation}
These variables are substituted into \eqref{sia:pureforhalfar}.  Note that the dome grows laterally ($x$ increases) as its height falls ($s$ decreases), which explains the signs of the powers.  After much calculus one finds that $t$ cancels from the equation if $\alpha=1/(3n+2)$, which is $\alpha=1/11$ when $n=3$.  Then an exact formula for $\phi(\tilde x)$ can be derived, which gives the invariant shape of the dome.  These formulas feature in the program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/dome.py}{\texttt{py/dome.py}}.

Figure \ref{fig:halfardome} was generated by changing a boolean flag in \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/dome.py}{\texttt{py/dome.py}}, so that the initial condition is equal to the Halfar dome formula, evaluated at its characteristic time $t_0$ \cite{Bueleretal2005}.  The simulation runs for the same duration as before, and then the program plots both the numerical and exact solutions at that time.  The fit is very close.

\onefigsize{halfardome}{Just like Figure \ref{fig:dome}, but starting from an initial Halfar dome.  The numerical and exact final states are very close.}{4.0in}

The power $\alpha=1/(3n+2)$ is a manifestation of shear-thinning water ice flow \cite{Nyeetal2000}.  The Halfar solution solves the problem of putting a Dirac delta mass of ice at the origin, at some conceptual starting time, and evolving its shape only through flow.  The dome expands laterally at $t^\alpha$ rate, which starts very fast when the dome is tall, but becomes agonizingly slow as $t$ gets large.  The geometry never reaches steady state.

\subsection*{Convergence and verification}  Our program \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/dome.py}{\texttt{py/dome.py}} calls \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/shallow.py}{\texttt{py/shallow.py}}, so the code is already complicated.  Are we sure that our implementation is correct?

These three justifications for numerical correctness appear in the modeling literature:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The author made no mistakes.
  \item The numerical results are close to those from other researchers.
  \item The numerical results are close to an exact solution.
\end{enumerate}
The first two justifications, \emph{infallibility} and \emph{intercomparison}, should be less common than they are.  The third, preferred by the CFD community \cite{Wesseling2001}, is the simple idea of \emph{verification}: the numerical error should be measured in cases where the correct answer is known.  Here ``correct answer'' means an exact solution to the PDE itself.

In technical terms, we intend our numerical schemes to \emph{converge} to the exact (but usually unknown) solution of the continuum problem under \emph{grid refinement}.  Proving such convergence is a main topic in numerical textbooks \cite{LeVeque2007,MortonMayers2005}.  However, even if we expect convergence to happen in theory, we still need to \emph{verify} it for the actual computer program which implements the scheme.  This involves measuring the accuracy of the scheme by finding its empirical rate of convergence in particular cases.  Almost any implementation mistake will break convergence, or drastically reduce its rate; the fragility of the verification process is its strongest feature.

In theory, if we had a linear diffusion equation like \eqref{diffusion:ske:planar} then scheme \eqref{fd:diffusion:planar} would make $O(\Delta t) + O((\Delta x)^2)$ numerical errors.  That is, one finds these rates for the \emph{local truncation error} \cite{MortonMayers2005} just from examining the FD formulas.  This is the best rate we can expect when measuring the \emph{numerical error} compared to the exact solution.  However, the convergence rate for glacier equations is reduced because of the singular-gradient margin shape.

For an experiment, set the resolution $J$ in \href{https://github.com/bueler/mccarthy/blob/master/py/dome.py}{\texttt{py/dome.py}} to five values $J=40$, $80$, $160$, $320$, $640$.  The measured errors against the Halfar solution (Figure \ref{fig:halfardome}) are as follows:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{r|c|c|c}
$J$ & $\Delta x$ (m) & maximum (m) & average (m) \\ \hline
40 & 1000 & 66.52 & 6.94 \\
80 & 500 & 7.24 & 1.79 \\
160 & 250 & 8.57 & 0.77 \\
320 & 125 & 15.63 & 0.36 \\
640 & 62.5 & 9.42 & 0.12
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two columns are the maximum and average numerical elevation errors at the final time.  The average error decreases with improved grid resolution, as expected for a correctly-implemented code, at a measured rate $O((\Delta x)^{1.40})$.  By contrast, because the numerical error in margin location will never be better than about one grid space \cite{Bueler2021conservation}, and because the margin gradient is unbounded,  the maximum error behaves erratically \cite{Bueleretal2005}.

The Halfar solution is simple to use for verification, but such elegant exact solutions are rare.  However, exact solutions for verification can actually be constructed at will, by the \emph{method of manufactured solutions} \cite{Wesseling2001}; it is worthwhile to learn this trick.
